# A 2.78-Trillion-Parameter Model Now Runs on a MacBook. Build for the Night Shift.

*August 4, 2026 — trend monitoring for independent builders and one-person companies*

---

## Key Insights

**The headline judgment:** Over the past two weeks, four unrelated projects put frontier-scale models onto hardware ordinary people already own. Not distilled. Not pruned. Full weights. They all arrived at the same trick — **keep the shared trunk resident in RAM, stream the MoE experts off SSD on demand** — and the result is that “can it run?” stopped being the question. “How fast?” is the question now.

That distinction is the entire opportunity, and most builders are about to get it wrong. At 0.45–0.62 tokens per second, a full frontier model cannot hold a conversation. It can absolutely finish a day’s worth of batch work overnight. **Stop building local chatbots. Start building local night shifts.** Pick the async framing and the slowness stops being a defect — it becomes a cost structure nobody with an API bill can match.

The same week, David Crawshaw published [*Devtools must be open source*](https://blog.exe.dev/devtools-must-be-open-source) — 172 points on HN — arguing that agents have inverted the ROI on personalizing software, because the maintenance tax that used to eat the benefit can now be handled by a nightly rebase job. Put the two together and you get a genuinely new shipping shape for a solo founder: **ship the repo, run it on the customer’s machine, against the customer’s weights.** No servers, no multi-tenant compliance surface, no data leaving the building.

**Four numbers that should change your decisions:**

- **29.06 GB of RAM opens a 2.78-trillion-parameter model.** [WASTE](https://github.com/sqliteai/waste) converts Kimi K3’s 1.42 TB published weights into a 982 GB container and decodes at 0.45–0.62 tok/s on a 64 GB M5 Pro MacBook. The same engine runs Kimi-Linear 48B from a 19 GB container, **1.28 GB minimum RAM, 10.65 tok/s** — that second row is the one you can actually build a product on.
- **8.24 GB peak RSS, a 176 KB engine, zero GPUs.** [kimi-k3-in-c](https://github.com/FareedKhan-dev/kimi-k3-in-c) runs the same 2.78T model in portable C99 at 32.69 seconds per token. Absurdly slow, and that is the point: it establishes the floor. **The barrier is no longer a GPU. It’s patience.**
- **14.3 GB compressed into a ~2 GB budget.** [TurboFieldfare](https://github.com/drumih/turbo-fieldfare) runs instruction-tuned Gemma 4 26B-A4B on 8 GB Apple Silicon Macs — 1.35 GB resident core, experts streamed per token, backed by 103 documented measurements. That’s a five-year-old laptop spec.
- **KV cache: 11.25 GB → 0.21 GB at 4K context.** Linear attention plus a compressed latent KV cache is the unglamorous reason local long-context went from impossible to routine. Architecture, not hardware, moved this one.

## Market Trends

### 1. Weight streaming became a real category, not a party trick

You can tell something is a trend when unrelated people build it simultaneously. Local inference is squarely in that state right now: a C99 engine doing 2.78T in 8.24 GB, a Swift/Metal runtime doing 26B in ~2 GB, an embeddable C engine (WASTE) streaming a 982 GB container off NVMe, `gavamedia/deltafin` (651 stars) packaging full K3 behind an OpenAI-compatible server for local chat and coding agents, and [AirLLM](https://github.com/lyogavin/airllm) hitting 118 points on HN for 70B inference on a 4 GB GPU.

The technique converges because the math does. A mixture-of-experts model activates roughly 4% of its parameters per token, so there is no reason to hold all of them in memory. Keep the shared trunk resident, read only the selected experts from disk, arrange the container so one expert is one aligned read, overlap those reads with compute, and turn whatever RAM is left into a bounded cache with a lookahead router prefetching the next layer.

What matters commercially is the elasticity. WASTE’s own measurements show that **shrinking the expert cache from 17.32 GB to 3.32 GB costs about 10% of throughput.** That is a remarkably flat curve — it means this approach degrades gracefully across a wide range of customer hardware instead of falling off a cliff, which is exactly what you need if you’re shipping software to machines you don’t control.

And the frontier is closing in behind it. Alibaba shipped **Qwen3.8-Max** on August 3 — 2.4 trillion parameters, tuned for coding and long-horizon work, 930 points and 480 comments on HN — with weights stated to open next week. The lag between “best closed model” and “runnable on your own hardware” is compressing toward weeks.

> **The constraint you cannot argue with:** 0.45–0.62 tok/s means a 500-token response takes over ten minutes. Every local product designed around synchronous chat is dead on arrival today. Every local product designed around a queue is viable today.

### 2. Agents made open-source distribution the economically rational choice

Crawshaw’s argument is concrete rather than ideological. Five years ago you didn’t fork your tools, because every upstream release meant a painful merge and the maintenance cost consumed the benefit. That cost line just went to roughly zero. Two prompts do it: build from source and record the motivation in version control, then **run a nightly job that fetches upstream, rebases local changes, verifies the software still works, and replaces the current version.**

Both prompts can be packaged as a skill and shipped inside the agent, so the user never writes them. The conclusion is sharp: closed-source tools just lost one of their load-bearing sales arguments. *“We handle maintenance for you”* is no longer scarce.

For a solo founder this unbundles distribution from hosting:

|  | Hosted SaaS | Repo-delivered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infra cost per customer | Scales with usage | **Zero** |
| Data residency | Your problem | Customer’s machine |
| Compliance surface | Multi-tenant, audited | Effectively none |
| Inference cost | Your API bill | Customer’s hardware |
| Support cost | Centralized, cheap | **Long-tail, expensive** |
| Pricing anchor | Usage tiers | License + updates |

The revealed-preference data supports it. Three of this fortnight’s fastest-growing repos are all install-and-modify artifacts, and their fork ratios say so: `microsoft/skill-recorder` (records a work session and reconstructs it as a reusable skill) at **1,311 stars / 132 forks — 10.1%** in five days; `yc-software/qm` (multiplayer agent harness) at **9,040 / 945 — 10.5%**; `trycompai/crm` (agentic-first open CRM) at **2,758 / 314 — 11.4%** in three days. People are cloning these, not bookmarking them.

Local weights and repo delivery are natural partners. The customer runs your source on their machine against their model. You sell the design, the updates, and the accountability — not resold compute.

### 3. Deterministic verification is turning into a shippable artifact

A [July 30 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28271) offers a template worth stealing outright. Migrating COBOL to Java, the authors refuse to let the model judge its own output. Instead they stand up **two runtime environments** — the COBOL source and the generated Java target, both instrumented with mocks and run off-mainframe on commodity hardware — and require deterministic parity: identical inputs must produce identical outputs. An agentic loop (“Locksmith Loop”) performs witness search over input mocks to penetrate branches, then applies parity-preserving mutations; when it stalls, an analyzer identifies the *locked paragraph* blocking deeper exploration. Across three programs from 430 to 4,114 lines, they hit near-complete coverage on the open-source cases and **91.90% branch coverage** on a production-like internal program, with every accepted test case matching the COBOL reference.

Set that beside the same week’s [hallucinated SQLite CVEs](https://research.jfrog.com/post/sqlite-critical-cves-or-llm-slops/) — 598 points on HN — where AI-generated advisories were rated Critical by NVD and CISA’s ADP, and were only unwound by JFrog doing unglamorous manual work: clone the official repo, diff against real source, compile in clean containers, run each PoC under AddressSanitizer.

Both stories say the same thing. **Self-reported model success is worth nothing. An external, reproducible referee is worth money.**

> **The test to apply to every feature you build:** is there a judge that does not depend on model judgment? Compilers are judges. Test suites are judges. Dual-environment parity checks are judges. AddressSanitizer is a judge. Where a judge exists, you can sell today. Where one doesn’t, you’re selling hope.

### 4. The productivity math still argues for narrow, verifiable products

Bjorn Roche’s [AI productivity gap](https://bjorg.bjornroche.com/management/ai-productivity-gap/) breaks a senior developer’s day down honestly and finds that even assuming AI makes coding 3× faster, the total saving is **1.25 hours out of eight — about 15%**. Juniors gain more (2 hours, ~25%) precisely because they spend more of the day writing code. Design, architecture, code review, meetings and admin barely move. And the one line item that *grows* post-AI is testing, CI/CD and deployment.

That last detail is the tell, and it lines up perfectly with the verification thread above: **the work AI creates is verification work.** If you’re picking a product to build, the categories that shrink with better models are risky; the categories that grow with more AI output are the safe bet.

## Build Directions

### Direction 1 — A local “night shift” agent runner *(strongest pick this issue)*

A task queue that lives on the customer’s own machine. Work goes in during the day — bulk refactors, document processing, data cleaning, report generation — runs overnight, and results are waiting in the morning. The pitch is not speed. It is **zero marginal inference cost and data that never leaves the box.**

**Stack:** WASTE or `deltafin` for a local OpenAI-compatible endpoint (use a mid-size model like Kimi-Linear 48B at 10.65 tok/s — frontier-scale at 0.6 tok/s won’t clear a batch) · Claude Code or Codex as the executor pointed at localhost · a dumb-simple queue (SQLite plus a daemon is enough) · every job must pass a deterministic check — tests green, diff reviewed, schema validated — before it’s marked done.  
**Cost/time:** 4–6 weeks to something usable, genuinely solo-shippable. Near-zero API spend during development. Customer hardware floor: 16–32 GB for mid-size models, 64 GB for frontier.  
**Pricing:** $30–120/seat/month, or perpetual license plus annual updates. For customers whose data legally cannot leave the network, $500+/month is defensible on compliance alone.

### Direction 2 — Repo-delivered vertical SaaS for privacy-bound industries

Pick a domain where the data genuinely cannot leave the building — law firm case files, clinic records, accounting workpapers, internal compensation. Don’t build a cloud service. Ship the whole system as a repository: local deployment, local model, local database. You’re selling domain logic and continuous updates, not hosting.

**Stack:** a mid-size local runtime (TurboFieldfare-class, runs on 8–16 GB machines) · SQLite or Postgres locally · Tauri or Electron shell · **bundle the nightly upstream-rebase skill** so customers never manage upgrades manually.  
**Cost/time:** 6–10 weeks to a first vertical. Zero server cost is the structural advantage — no cloud bill means a completely different margin profile from your hosted competitors.  
**Pricing:** $200–800/month per organization, or annual license. Compliance is the pricing anchor, not feature count.

### Direction 3 — Local/cloud routing and cost governance

Every task asks two questions: can it tolerate latency, and can the data leave? Route to local weights or cloud API accordingly, and account for every dollar. `mikehasa/agentacct` (545 stars — breaks each agent task into tools used, files changed, tests run, time and tokens, local-first, no login, no telemetry) proves the visibility demand exists. **Routing plus cost control is still unclaimed.**

**Stack:** an OpenAI-compatible proxy in Go or Rust (a few hundred lines to start) · local backends via WASTE / deltafin / Ollama · YAML policy file expressing rules by data classification, latency budget and cost ceiling · OTel-style trace capture with a local dashboard.  
**Cost/time:** 3–5 weeks to a credible CLI. Open-source the core for distribution.  
**Pricing:** free CLI, $15–40/seat/month for the team policy layer and audit reporting.

### Direction 4 — Cross-agent screen-recording-to-skill

Microsoft validated the demand — `skill-recorder` hit 1,311 stars in five days — but it only exports into the Copilot ecosystem. **The open version targeting Claude Code, Codex and OpenClaw is empty space.** Record a real work session, reconstruct it as intent plus ordered steps, export to a portable skill format, and add the layer Microsoft didn’t: **auto-generate an executable assertion for each step** so the skill verifies itself instead of just replaying.

**Stack:** Tauri or Electron capture client · screen frames plus input events fed to a vision model for step extraction · standard skill directory output · assertions in a small DSL that runs before and after each step.  
**Cost/time:** 5–8 weeks. Step extraction is the hard part; once it works the leverage is large.  
**Pricing:** free for individuals, $10–25/seat/month for shared team skill libraries.

### Direction 5 — Legacy migration sold as an equivalence proof

Don’t sell “AI migrates your code.” Sell **evidence of behavioral equivalence.** Follow the dual-environment parity method: stand up sandboxes for source and target, require identical outputs from identical mocked inputs, and let an agent loop do witness search to reach deep branches. The deliverable is a coverage report and a parity attestation. The migrated code is a byproduct.

**Stack:** Docker for both runtime environments · an input mock generator · native branch-coverage tooling per language · an agent loop for witness search · auditable report output.  
**Cost/time:** 8–12 weeks; narrow v1 to a single language pair (COBOL→Java, VB6→C#, or Perl→Python — pick one).  
**Pricing:** project-based, $15k–80k per engagement. The buyer is whoever gets blamed if the migration breaks production.

## Risk Factors

- **The speed limit is real — don’t rationalize it.** Frontier models at ~0.6 tok/s cannot serve any experience where a user waits. If your product isn’t async by design, your first cohort churns before week two.
- **Hardware requirements will disqualify half your market.** 64 GB is the practical floor for frontier-scale local; 32 GB opens the model but pages heavily. Mid-size models in the 20B–50B band reach vastly more machines — start there and grow up, not down.
- **“Open weights” is not a license review.** The distance between an open-weights announcement and Apache 2.0 covers a lot of ground. Read the actual terms before shipping to enterprise, especially if you’re redistributing the model inside your product.
- **One platform release can turn you into a checkbox.** Apple and Microsoft are both moving local runtimes into the OS. Your moat has to be domain logic, verification rules and delivery evidence — never “I can make a model run locally.”
- **Repo delivery has a brutal support tail.** Customer machines vary wildly, and debugging a local deployment costs far more than debugging your own cluster. Price it in from day one or support tickets will quietly eat the margin advantage you gained by dropping servers.

## Action Recommendations

**If you are starting out:**

- ✅ Install a local inference engine this week and benchmark a 20B–50B model on your actual hardware. **You have no product decisions to make until you know your own tok/s.**
- ✅ Take one task in your current workflow whose result can wait until morning and move it to a local overnight queue. That’s your first prototype, and you’re the first customer.
- ✅ Default your next product to repo delivery rather than hosting. Price both shapes on paper before you decide — the numbers often aren’t close.
- ✅ Add the nightly upstream-rebase job to your own agent today. It’s the single most copyable idea from Crawshaw’s piece.
- ✅ For every agent output, name the judge. If you can’t name one that’s independent of the model, pick a different problem.

**If you have a team or an existing product:**

- ✅ Audit your inference bill and isolate the latency-insensitive, batchable portion. Model what it costs to move that slice to local weights — it’s usually the largest line item.
- ✅ Go back to the deals you lost to “our data can’t leave the network” and quote them a local deployment. That demand always existed; the technology only just caught up.
- ✅ Count how many of your constraints are enforced by prompt text. Migrate the ones touching money, data access or irreversible actions into code checks first.
- ✅ Make local-vs-cloud a configurable routing layer rather than a hardcoded choice, or the next pricing shift makes you rewrite it.
- ✅ Verify the actual license terms of every open-weight model you depend on now, before an enterprise legal team asks.

## What It Means

The cost wall came down twice this year. First inference prices collapsed, and the winners were the people who shipped fastest. Now the hardware wall has come down too — and this one changes the *shape* of the product, not just the margin. When the model can run on the customer’s laptop, hosting stops being a requirement and starts being a choice, and every argument that used to justify a cloud subscription has to be re-earned.

The trap is obvious and everyone is going to fall into it: building a local chatbot, discovering it takes eleven minutes to answer, and concluding the technology isn’t ready. The technology is ready. It’s ready for work that doesn’t watch the clock. Batch jobs, overnight pipelines, background processing, anything where the answer being correct matters far more than the answer being immediate — that’s a large category, and it’s currently paying API prices for the privilege of being fast at something that never needed to be.

Combine that with agents making open-source delivery economically sane, and with the week’s other clear lesson — that verification, not generation, is the scarce good — and the shape of a durable one-person product comes into focus. **Ship the repo. Run it on their hardware. Let it work overnight. Prove it was correct in the morning.**
