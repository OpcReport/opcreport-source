# Generating Ten Math Breakthroughs Cost $2,000. Verifying Them Still Costs a PhD.

*August 4, 2026 — trend monitoring for independent builders and one-person companies*

---

## Key Insights

**The headline judgment:** Three posts sat in the Hacker News top ten today that look unrelated. OpenAI announced ten new results in mathematics and theoretical computer science produced by its models, and researcher Noam Brown added the cost on X: **the combined API spend for all ten was under $2,000.** The same day, a **475-point** post described an engineer forcing himself to **manually retype every line of LLM-generated code**. A **901-point** post argued that model output quality is **almost entirely a function of the questioner’s domain depth.**

Read together they say one thing: **the price of generating has collapsed toward zero, and the price of confirming it’s correct has not moved at all.** Two thousand dollars bought ten proofs — but Brown also conceded they tried other major problems without success, and never disclosed how many problems were attempted in total. And the results count as results only because mathematicians formalized them in Lean and signed their names to the correctness. The sharpest line in the thread: **“You probably need to do the hard grunt work by hand first to develop the skills and intuition to be able to verify an AI-generated result.”**

For a one-person company this is a pricing problem, not a philosophical one. What you can conjure for $2,000, your customer can conjure too. **Being able to prove it’s right — and being willing to carry responsibility for that judgment — is the part someone pays for.** The corroborating signal is blunt: the tools that spiked this week aren’t generation tools. **They’re checkers.**

**Five numbers that should change your decisions:**

- **Under $2,000 for ten advances in mathematics and theoretical computer science** — total, at GPT-5.6 Sol API prices. But Brown’s own follow-ups matter more than the headline: **“we did try other major problems without success”** and **“we didn’t spend a lot on each problem — it’s possible to push test-time compute much further.”** Circumstantial reading in the thread puts per-problem budgets around **$200–500.** The correct interpretation is not “AI is cheap.” It’s that **the cost of a single attempt is now low enough to permit mass trial-and-error, which moves the entire cost center to selection and verification.**
- **2x, not 10x.** Ankur Sethi’s honest self-assessment after months of a workflow where the agent writes code only in chat and he types every line in by hand. **Use this number for client expectation-setting; it’s more credible than any vendor’s.** What he buys with the lost speed: catching hallucinations, refactoring as he goes, and above all **a spatial map of his own codebase.**
- **ratchet: 428 stars in 4 days. humanizer-cli: 564 stars in 3 days.** The first is a `PostToolUse` hook that measures whether your agent obeyed its rules while it’s still working. The second is “33 ways to spot AI-written text” in your terminal. **Both are pure verification. Neither generates anything. That’s what’s getting starred.**
- **$1.65T in hidden debt; bond issuance up 973.7% year over year.** S&P Global counts **$225B** issued by hyperscalers so far in 2026, on pace for $400B. S&P’s own words: market participants are “growing leery of quickly rising leverage.” **The only implication you need: today’s cheap token price is partly subsidized by borrowing. Don’t underwrite your gross margin on it staying flat forever.**
- **2.78 trillion parameters on a single CPU in 8.24 GB of RAM.** `kimi-k3-in-c`, 1,475 stars in three days, portable C99, no BLAS, no framework, no GPU. Plus Swiftlet running an 80B on a Mac in 4.3 GB. **This is the hedge against the line above. Build the fallback path now.**

## Market Trends

### One: cognitive debt surfaced, and the industry started pricing “I don’t understand my own codebase”

Ankur Sethi’s post drew 475 points and 383 comments not because the technique is clever but because he said out loud what everyone is experiencing and nobody admits. His framing is direct: letting an agent one-shot an entire feature leaves him “unsatisfied and disoriented.” And reviewing AI-generated PRs — “poring over hundreds of lines of overly-defensive, badly-commented, subtly incorrect code” — is something he’ll grudgingly do for an employer, **“while making sure said employer becomes an ex-employer as soon as possible,”** but never for his own projects.

His fix is almost comically low-tech. He hard-codes an instruction into the agents file of every personal project: **never create, edit, move, rename, or delete files; show every proposed edit in chat so he can type it in manually.** Same for commands that modify repository state — print them, he’ll run them. And a closing line: “I’m an experienced developer. Do not explain syntax, APIs, programming concepts.”

What’s notable is that every benefit he lists is about **preserving human capability**, not code quality. Typing forces him to slow down, which makes hallucinations and bad design choices visible. He cleans up and refactors to his own taste as he goes. And most importantly he builds **a spatial map of the codebase — he knows where every piece of functionality lives, so when a change is needed he knows exactly where it goes, which in turn makes him better at instructing the LLM later.** He compares it explicitly to the old advice given to teenage programmers: **never copy-paste from a book; type the examples out.**

> **Three real boundaries the comment thread established**
>
> **Boundary one: the speed cost is real.** A commenter pushed back — “you move way slowly even on personal projects, you won’t even get the basic UI done in a few days, is that OK?” The reply was equally direct: **“It is meant to offset not knowing why everything degrades and you can’t make progress after the first month.”** Neither is wrong. You’re trading present speed for future maintainability, and the right answer depends on project lifespan.
>
> **Boundary two: there’s a cheaper substitute.** The most useful counter came from `_davide_`: **“there is a simpler way, make a complete mental model of the changes and ask questions to confirm your understanding. so much faster.”** And `dsign` supplied the best worked pattern — needing undocumented SIMD instructions on an ESP32-P4, he had an LLM reverse-engineer the ISA into a `SIMD_P4.md` document, **then hand-writes the assembly himself and asks the LLM to refine the doc when an op is unclear. The understanding stays in his head; the grunt work leaves.**
>
> **Boundary three: in a team, this logic needs a different implementation.** One commenter asked whether a human really loses much context if the codebase has human-driven test suites, quality evals, robust documentation, and modular implementation. **The question is the point: preventing cognitive debt is a discipline problem solo and an engineering-constraint problem in a team. Which leads directly to the next trend.**

### Two: rules stopped relying on good intentions — checkers are closing the loop

Where Sethi relies on self-discipline, a cluster of tools spiking this week takes the opposite position: **assume discipline fails, then measure.**

`0xwilliamortiz/ratchet`, created July 31, 428 stars in four days, opens its README by naming the problem: **“Every minimalism ruleset for coding agents is an open loop.”** You inject *prefer the standard library, do not add dependencies, keep the diff small*, the model reads it, **and then nothing checks whether any of it happened. Compliance is assumed.** And the model drifts over a long session — that’s expected, not exceptional — **and nobody finds out until review.**

Its approach: the ruleset still goes in, but a `PostToolUse` hook **reads every edit the agent makes, measures it, and reports the findings back into the same session while the agent is still working.** The README demo is vivid. You ask for a date picker on the settings page; the agent installs flatpickr, writes a wrapper component, adds a stylesheet. The hook immediately fires: `+71 -0 lines (net +71/150)`, `2/3 new files`, `1/1 new deps` — new dependency flatpickr, *justify it against the stdlib and the platform, or drop it*; `<input type="date">` likely sufficient; DatePicker only forwards to Flatpickr, *call it directly and delete the wrapper*. **The agent reverts and ships the native control.** The author’s summary is the line worth keeping: **“Nothing here depends on the model having felt persuaded by a system prompt.”**

Its three modes are numbers, not adjectives. And one design detail proves real-world use: **first run accepts everything already in the codebase as a baseline, so only new findings fire.**

| Mode | New files | New deps | Net added lines | On overrun |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `advise` | 8 | 3 | 400 | findings only |
| `guard` | 3 | 1 | 150 | findings + budget warnings |
| `strict` | 1 | 0 | 60 | **the edit is blocked** |

> **Same day, same author, another 564 stars**
>
> On August 1 the same developer shipped `humanizer-cli` — **“33 ways to spot AI-written text, right in your terminal,”** with before/after examples and a draft checker, zero dependencies, 564 stars in three days.
>
> Put next to ratchet, they describe one emerging category: **generate nothing, just answer “does this meet the bar?”** And note the shared shape — **solo projects, zero dependencies, traction within days. This is currently the most accessible form for a one-person company: small, sharp, and solving one judgment call everybody needs and nobody wants to make by hand.**

### Three: the expertise premium — same model, different asker, different universe

Sean Goedecke’s 901-point *LLMs reward expertise* supplies the theory under both trends above: **everyone holds the same model, and the entire delta is the questioner’s domain depth.** OpenAI’s math results are the most expensive demonstration of that argument to date.

Unpack the details. **The $2,000 is real, but what it bought has to be underwritten by humans.** OpenAI’s own phrasing: “We helped prepare the manuscripts and formalize the proofs in Lean, **and we take responsibility for their correctness**“ — alongside an explicit refusal to claim human authorship for AI-generated proofs, on the grounds that doing so “would misrepresent both the system’s contribution and the nature of genuine human intellectual work.” The companion repo `openai/ten-proofs` (Lean certificates, 451 stars) is where that responsibility physically lives.

Commenters who know the tooling spelled out how heavy that responsibility is. Lean has escape hatches like `sorry` that let a “proof” pass vacuously, so **somebody has to verify none were used.** More fundamentally, **the formalized theorem statements themselves must be human-checked to confirm they authentically mirror the real theorems** — a flawless proof of a subtly wrong statement is worthless. And the thread surfaced a live incident from last week: **a bug in the Lean kernel was discovered by way of an LLM tricking both itself and its handler into believing it had non-constructively proven the existence of a nontrivial Collatz cycle.**

> **Don’t misread this as “so AI doesn’t work”**
>
> The cost structure changed, that’s all. One commenter did the honest arithmetic: real total cost = **hours to write the prompt + hours to check the result + API spend.** For a university researcher that’s a choice between a grad student (several tens of thousands of dollars) and a prompt plus roughly $2,000 of inference plus a few hours on harness and review. **The second wins overwhelmingly — provided you’re capable of those few hours of review.**
>
> **Translated for a one-person company: restructure your invoice.** Generation can be nearly free, because customers will figure out its price soon enough. **Charge for precision of questioning, for filtering the failed attempts, and for the sentence “I confirm this is correct.” All three are expertise, and expertise is the one input this cycle hasn’t deflated.**

## Build Directions

Five directions, one thread: **once generation is free, the money moved to proving it’s right and to protecting human understanding.**

### Direction 1 — Sell a verification loop to a regulated industry (top pick this issue)

**What:** Take ratchet’s mechanic — hook measures in real time, violations fed back mid-session — and turn it into a compliance loop for one specific regulated context. Not generic code style. **Finance, healthcare, payments — places that must demonstrate “we actually followed the process.”** Every code change touching patient data carries a redaction-check record; every payment-path change carries test-coverage evidence. **You aren’t selling the blocking. You’re selling the evidence chain someone can hand to an auditor.**

**Why now:** 428 stars in four days proves developers accept the mechanic — but ratchet is generic, free, MIT. **Generic tools can’t charge. “Get you through the audit” can.** And the need grows with the share of code written by agents: **compliance will eventually ask who wrote this, under what rules, and who verified it. Right now nobody can answer.**

**Stack:** Claude Code / Codex hook mechanisms as the capture layer (`PostToolUse` is the ready-made entry point) · express rules as executable assertions, never natural language · evidence into an append-only log (signed commits in a git repo is enough) · Next.js for the audit view · **deploy inside the customer’s own cloud — this buyer will not let the data leave.**

**Cost/time:** MVP 6–8 weeks; near-zero infra (runs on customer hardware). Price **$500–2,500/month per team**, or **$5,000–20,000 per audit cycle**. **The only real barrier is knowing what that industry’s auditors actually want — so only build for an industry you’ve worked in.**

### Direction 2 — Codebase spatial maps as a service

**What:** The asset Sethi buys with manual retyping is the spatial map — **knowing where every piece of functionality lives.** Productize it. Against a codebase the customer’s agents have half-written, produce a **continuously updated structural map**: module boundaries, data flow, ownership and last substantive change per area, and flags for “changes here are high-risk.” **The deliverable isn’t documentation. It’s a living map that humans and agents can both read.**

**Why now:** The comment-thread rebuttal already stated the demand: **a codebase with strong tests, good docs, and modular structure doesn’t cost humans their context.** Most agent-accelerated codebases have none of the three. **This is a problem manufactured by AI coding itself, and its scale grows daily.** And agents are the right tool to fix it — point agents at what agents wrote.

**Stack:** Claude Code / Codex for repo traversal and structure extraction · output as `AGENTS.md` plus per-module skill files, **so it is simultaneously human documentation and agent context** · git history analysis to locate churn zones and risk areas · CI job to re-run and keep it fresh · **deliver as a pull request, straight into the customer’s repo.**

**Cost/time:** 3–5 weeks to build the pipeline, then 2–5 days per customer. Price **$2,000–8,000 one-time** plus **$99–399/month** to keep it current. **Clear acquisition filter: find companies whose headcount is flat and whose codebase doubled in six months.**

### Direction 3 — Local inference fallback, as a hedge against token repricing

**What:** For small teams whose business processes now sit on hosted APIs, build a **local/self-hosted fallback inference path**: move batch jobs, overnight runs, and privacy-sensitive work onto local models, keep the small fraction that genuinely needs low latency or frontier capability on the cloud. Deliver a switchable routing config plus an acceptance test suite.

**Why now:** Both sides of the signal arrived at once. Capability is sinking — `kimi-k3-in-c` runs a **2.78-trillion-parameter model on a single CPU in 8.24 GB** of RAM in portable C99, 1,475 stars in three days; Swiftlet does **80B in 4.3 GB on a Mac**, 35B on an iPhone. And price risk is rising — **hyperscalers have issued $225B in bonds this year, up 973.7%, with $1.65T in hidden debt**, and S&P says the market is growing leery. **Today’s token price contains borrowed subsidy, and that gets settled eventually.**

**Stack:** minimal local runtimes — llama.cpp, Swiftlet — **skip the heavy frameworks** · write the router yourself, it’s one function, dispatch by task class · the critical piece is the **acceptance test set proving local output is equivalent to cloud output for this specific job** — without it no customer will switch · start on the customer’s existing idle hardware, add a GPU only if needed.

**Cost/time:** 4–6 weeks. Price **$3,000–12,000 one-time** plus optional **$200–600/month** managed. **State the tradeoff explicitly: the cost is latency, not capability.** Unbeatable for batch; do not touch real-time interactive with it.

### Direction 4 — Expert eval sets: turn domain judgment into a deliverable

**What:** Find a genuine expert — customs broker, clinical coder, tax specialist, bid writer — and **extract their judgment criteria rather than their workflow**: what counts as anomalous, what must be double-checked, where the commonsense answer is wrong. Package it as an **eval set** and sell it to people in that industry already using AI who have no way to know whether their output is sound.

**Why now:** Goedecke’s 901-point argument is exactly this: **same model, all the delta is domain depth.** His illustration is Terence Tao’s ChatGPT transcript on a Jacobian Conjecture counterexample — short messages, expertise signalled early so the model drops into talking-to-mathematicians mode, pushback phrased as **“this looks more complex than I was hoping for”** rather than direct contradiction, and Tao almost never taking the model’s suggestion about where to go next. **None of that transmits through a generic prompting tutorial. It only transmits as concrete criteria for what counts as wrong in a specific field — and the natural carrier for criteria is an eval set.**

**Stack:** promptfoo or Inspect as the eval framework · criteria as plain Markdown skill files, **no programming required** · ship with before/after numbers — **“X before, Y after” is the only thing that convinces a buyer** · Gumroad or Lemon Squeezy for checkout.

**Cost/time:** 2–3 weeks, most of it spent extracting what’s already in the expert’s head; near-zero running cost. Price **$199–899 one-time** or **$39/month** with updates. **Single go/no-go test: can you produce eval numbers? If not, don’t ship.**

### Direction 5 — AI-text attestation for people who must prove a human wrote it

**What:** Turn `humanizer-cli`-class detection into a compliance service for one specific context. **Not a general-purpose detector** — that lane is both crowded and untrustworthy. Target contexts with **hard disclosure obligations or reputational exposure**: publisher submission screening, law-firm outbound documents, journal pre-review, brand acceptance of outsourced content. Deliver an archivable check report plus a human review opinion.

**Why now:** `humanizer-cli` hit 564 stars in three days as a **zero-dependency CLI**, which tells you the underlying capability is already commoditized — **so don’t sell the tool, sell the judgment and the liability.** Meanwhile demand is hardening: more institutions need a written account of how a document was produced. **Same logic as Direction 1: the market is paying for provable, not for generative.**

**Stack:** open-source detectors for first-pass screening — **tell the customer plainly this is screening, never claim an accuracy rate** · add a domain rule layer for how humans in that specific field actually write · fixed, archivable, traceable report template · **never skip the human review step — it is your entire differentiation from a free tool** · simple web upload and queue, nothing more.

**Cost/time:** 3–4 weeks. Price **$20–100 per item** or **$299–999/month** for volume. **Biggest risk is overclaiming: the contract must state this is advisory judgment, not forensic determination, or your first false positive ends the business.**

## Risk Factors

- **The “$2,000” figure is already being misused. Don’t quote it to clients.** Brown himself conceded other major problems failed, and never disclosed total problems attempted, attempts per problem, or harness cost. One commenter compared the selective disclosure to p-hacking. **All you can honestly tell a client: single attempts are cheap, so mass trial-and-error is now viable — not “this class of problem costs two grand.”**
- **Verification tools become open loops too.** ratchet’s `strict` mode blocks edits outright, which sounds great until it fires often enough that the human’s first instinct is to disable it rather than comply. **If you build Direction 1, your hard problem isn’t detection — it’s making people not want to route around it. That’s ten times harder than the engineering.**
- **The manual-retyping discipline loses money on client work.** Sethi is explicit that this is a **personal-project** practice, and equally explicit that reviewing AI PRs for an employer is something he does grudgingly while planning his exit. **2x doesn’t survive contact with an invoice. On paid work, comprehension has to be protected by test suites and structural constraints, not by billable hours.**
- **Cheap inference is borrowed, not earned.** $225B issued, up 973.7%, $1.65T hidden — against a US federal deficit near $2T this fiscal year and a Fed that is no longer a major Treasury buyer. **Capital Economics notes that if H1 trends hold, total bond issuance as a share of GDP will exceed any year on record outside the pandemic. Do not build your gross margin on token prices never rising.**

## Action Recommendations

**If you’re just starting out**

- ✅ **Spend two hours this week instrumenting yourself.** Take a project you’re pushing with an agent, write down three hard metrics (new dependencies, lines per change, new files), and actually hook them up and measure. **Not to productize — to see with your own eyes how far your agent drifts. That number will tell you whether Direction 1 is for you.**
- ✅ **Make Direction 4 your first product.** Two to three weeks, no infrastructure, and it directly tests whether you hold a real knowledge asymmetry. **If you can’t produce eval numbers, the answer is to go deeper into a domain, not to write more code.**
- ✅ **Copy dsign’s move, not Sethi’s.** Have the LLM chew through documentation, reverse-engineer, and produce reference material — **then build the core part yourself.** Understanding stays with you, grunt work leaves. Far better return than retyping line by line.
- ✅ **Stand up a local inference path this week, even a toy one.** Start with `kimi-k3-in-c` or Swiftlet and migrate one real batch job. **Not to save money today — so you have somewhere to go when prices actually move.**

**If you already have a product or clients**

- ✅ **Restructure your invoice.** Push the “generation” line item down or give it away, and move the money onto verification, filtering, and accountability. **Customers will discover generation is cheap. Better to relocate your value anchor yourself than to be negotiated down to it.**
- ✅ **Attach archivable verification evidence to every deliverable.** Test results, comparison data, review records. **OpenAI opened an entire Lean repository to carry the sentence “we take responsibility for their correctness.” What entitles your deliverable to ship with nothing but “it works”?**
- ✅ **Audit who on your team still understands the core modules.** If the answer is “nobody, agents wrote it,” that’s the problem Direction 2 solves for other people — **and it’s already growing inside your own company. Build living maps for your two or three most critical modules first.**
- ✅ **Run a token cost stress test.** Assume your primary model doubles in price next year: what’s left of your margin, and which features go underwater? **That number tells you exactly which calls to move local first.**

## What It Means

The comfortable version of this week’s story is that intelligence got cheap. The accurate version is narrower: **generation got cheap, and everything downstream of generation got relatively more expensive.**

Ten mathematical results for less than the price of a used laptop — but they required mathematicians to formalize, to check that no `sorry` slipped through, to confirm the formal statements matched the real ones, and to sign their names. That labor didn’t get cheaper. It got scarcer, because fewer people are practising the skills that produce it. That’s what “cognitive debt” actually names, and why a post about retyping code by hand drew 475 points on the same day.

**So the question for a one-person company isn’t “what can I generate?” It’s “what can I vouch for?”** The tools trending this week already answered it in their own way: nobody’s starring another generator. They’re starring the things that check.

**Sell the confirmation, not the output.**
