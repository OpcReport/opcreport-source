# Generation Went Free, Verification Didn’t: Fake AI CVEs Fooled NVD While a Worm Signed Itself Legitimate

**The short version:** Three stories this week look unrelated. JFrog demonstrated that a batch of SQLite vulnerabilities rated *critical* by NVD were fabricated by an LLM. A worm compromised 444 npm packages in about a day — over 2 billion monthly installs combined — and every poisoned version shipped with **valid GitHub Actions provenance**. Meanwhile the two most-discussed technical posts on Hacker News were “Don’t be a meat proxy” and “LLMs reward expertise.”

They are the same story. **The cost of producing something polished collapsed to zero. The cost of verifying it did not.** Every system that treated professional-looking output as evidence that a human did the work started failing at once.

---

## Key Insights

**The headline judgment:** the debate about whether AI can produce good work is over — it can, cheaply, in unlimited quantity. The consequential change is that **polish has been destroyed as a signal.** A well-formatted vulnerability advisory with precise function names, specific line numbers, and a runnable PoC used to mean somebody spent two weeks reverse-engineering. Now it means somebody spent two dollars. NVD accepted it. CISA’s ADP concurred. Red Hat scored one a 10.0. The entire authority chain was fooled by the same signal, because the entire chain was built on the assumption that generation is expensive.

For a one-person business the implication cuts both ways, sharply: **if your product’s value is generating content, your moat is going to zero this quarter. If your product’s value is proving something is real, you’re facing demand that just detonated.** The human-side corollary is the top technical post of the week — models don’t flatten the gap between expert and amateur, they **widen** it, because only the expert can tell whether the output is right.

## Market Trends

### 1. Verification is the new bottleneck, and the institutions that were supposed to do it are failing simultaneously

The JFrog details matter, because the devil lives in them. In late July a freshly created GitHub repo published a batch of SQLite advisories. NVD rated them critical quickly; CISA’s ADP agreed. Then JFrog tried to reproduce them and the whole thing disintegrated:

- `exprComputeOperands()`, the function at the center of one advisory, **did not exist in SQLite 3.41** — it was added mid-2025.
- `sqlite3ReleaseTempReg()` was claimed to cause a use-after-free, but it merely recycles register indices into an array. **A UAF is impossible there by design.**
- One advisory claimed a fix landed in 3.51.3. The diff between 3.51.2 and 3.51.3 shows **zero changes to `src/expr.c`**. The patch was invented.
- Cited line numbers 3,555 and 3,575 in `json.c` — a file that is **2,706 lines long** in that version.
- PoCs either returned normal output or died at the SQL parser, never reaching the alleged bug.

This is not a story about an AI making a mistake. It’s a story about **a human review pipeline being defeated by a sufficiently attractive document.** Reviewers saw correct formatting, precise symbols, specific line numbers, an executable PoC — all reliable proxies for diligence in 2023. Producing those proxies now costs nothing. Verifying them costs exactly what it always did: clone, checkout the tag, compile in a clean container, run under AddressSanitizer. JFrog did that. It cost researcher-days.

The npm attack proves the same point from the opposite direction. An attacker took over the `keyv` maintainer’s GitHub account, added `setup.mjs` plus a `"preinstall": "node setup.mjs"` hook to each `package.json`, pushed straight to `main`, and immediately cut releases. Because that is the *legitimate* publishing path, **every malicious version was signed with valid provenance by GitHub Actions.** By August 5 the worm had self-propagated to 444 packages across 1,381 versions, sweeping in official packages from Deliveroo, Picsart, and Qlik.

Put both together and an uncomfortable conclusion surfaces: **every automated trust primitive we built over the last decade — CVE IDs, CVSS scores, package signatures, provenance attestations — verifies formal correctness, not substantive truth.** When generation was expensive those correlated tightly, so the shortcut was rational. They have now decoupled.

> **What this means for you:** if you sell content generation of any kind — copy, code, reports, design — a competitor will offer ten times the volume at a tenth of the price next quarter. But if you can supply *evidence that something was actually verified*, you are in a market where supply just collapsed and demand just spiked. This isn’t a moral claim, it’s a supply-and-demand claim: pricing power lives on the scarce side, and the scarce side just changed.

### 2. Expertise gets amplified, not flattened — and that’s good news for independents

Sean Goedecke’s “LLMs reward expertise” drew 1,362 points and 445 comments, the most seriously discussed technical piece of the week. His argument is counterintuitive but holds: the popular view says LLMs make everyone a generalist, therefore prompting has no skill component, therefore skilled prompters get the same results as first-timers. He says that’s wrong — **the most important skill in prompting is domain expertise in what you’re prompting about.**

His example is Terence Tao’s public conversation with ChatGPT about the Jacobian Conjecture counterexample. The observations are concrete:

- Tao’s messages are **very short**, responding to the gist rather than point-by-point.
- The model’s replies to him are **far more concise** than for a typical user — by signaling expertise, Tao shunts it into “talking to mathematicians” mode instead of “explaining to amateurs” mode.
- He pushes back without flatly contradicting: *“this looks more complex than I was hoping for.”*
- He makes the leaps himself and **almost never takes the model’s advice about where to go next.**

But Goedecke stresses you can’t acquire this by copying the tips, because the core of the technique is *actually understanding the mathematics* — pulling the one relevant idea out of a multi-paragraph reply, proposing alternate formulations, noticing what “looks weird.” He reports the same in his own work: with a good theory of your codebase you can push the model much harder, because you can say “no, this could be simpler,” or “don’t we already do X?”

This locks into the first trend perfectly. Verification requires expertise, and expertise is now the lever. His conclusion is worth keeping verbatim: **for many tasks the human is the bottleneck, not the model, because the hard part is communicating exactly what kind of solution you want. The information is already in the model; it takes a smart human to pull it out.**

The week’s #1 post, “Don’t be a meat proxy,” is the same coin’s other face. The author is tired of receiving *“Claude said: [giant response verbatim]”* in Slack, PR reviews, and group chats. His response is blunt: I can talk to Claude myself, faster, and I get to control the context — I don’t need a meat proxy in between. He also names the review death spiral: paste the ticket into Claude Code, open a PR without reading the code, paste reviewer feedback back in, iterate. It ships. But **the reviewers did the implementation, using Claude Code, with you as the relay.**

MIT Sloan’s July research on AI financial advice supplies a third data point: the advice is surprisingly good overall, but **prompt differences produce systematically different advice depending on the user’s gender, financial literacy, and LLM familiarity.** Same model, different human, materially different answer — the exact inverse of “everyone gets the same output.”

### 3. The ROI on personalizing software was rewritten — with one condition: it must be open source

Tailscale co-founder David Crawshaw published “Devtools must be open source” on August 2 (716 points). His entry point is an observation from five years of asking engineers what software they’d written for themselves: almost none had. The reason was economic — the return was always questionable, there was always something more urgent, and **coming back to a personal project a year later for maintenance was extraordinarily painful.**

He argues that changed, via two specific prompts. First: *download the source, build it for local use, treat all future changes as source changes, and record the original motivation in version control.* Second, and more important:

> Set up a nightly cron job that executes the prompt: fetch upstream changes, rebase all local changes on top of upstream, check that the software works as intended, and replace the current version.

The second prompt is the hinge. **Agents don’t just write a one-off patch — they can own the ongoing job of staying synced with upstream**, which is precisely the cost that made personalization irrational. Startup cost fell and maintenance cost fell simultaneously, so the ROI curve changed shape rather than just shifting.

His own example shows the magnitude. He built `meat`, a tool that uses LLMs to strip the unimportant parts out of a diff. His reasoning is a sharp observation in its own right: models are now **more diligent than humans at rote correctness** — nil checks, error handling, import blocks — so reviewing those lines has no value. Model errors cluster in *architecture, unexpected use cases, and visual output their test environment never feeds back.* He wanted `meat` built into his editor and pre-processing commits in the background the instant they’re created, so he never waits. **One prompt did it.** His comparison lands: imagine the convoluted misery of plugging that into the VS Code extension API.

The commercial implication for independents is double-edged. Upside: you can now maintain a deeply customized toolchain for the price of a nightly cron. Downside: **this only works on open source.** In the same week Cursor quietly removed dollar-cost information from its usage page and CSV export (336 points, 154 comments), with long-time paying users openly discussing migration. Cursor’s staff explanation was an accidental feature-flag cleanup, but users noted the cost ring indicator next to the context gauge also vanished. One comment captures the stakes: **it is now much easier to leave an expensive model enabled and not notice until you’ve burned through your credits, and it’s hard to imagine that wasn’t the point.** A closed tool’s pricing can become opaque whenever the vendor likes, and you can’t fork your way out.

## Build Directions

### Direction 1 — Reproduction-first advisory verification (demand just detonated)

**What to build:** a CVE credibility triage service. Input an advisory, automatically run the JFrog playbook: clone the repo, checkout the claimed version tag, **statically verify the cited functions and line numbers actually exist** (this alone kills most of this week’s fakes), compile in Docker, run the PoC under ASan, and emit a three-state verdict — reproduced / not reproduced / cited symbols nonexistent.

The critical design constraint: this is **not “use AI to judge truth,” it’s “use deterministic execution to replace human reading.”** That’s the selling point — nobody will trust one AI auditing another.

**Stack:** GitHub API for sources; Docker sandboxes for builds (devcontainer spec for multi-language); ASan/Valgrind for memory checks; tree-sitter for symbol-existence checks — zero cost, zero LLM, and it filters most slop; a coding agent only to adapt PoCs into runnable form. Price per verified advisory, not per API call.

**Cost and timeline:** $50–200/month sandbox compute; 6–8 weeks solo for a C/C++ plus JS MVP. The customer profile is unusually clear — security teams, distro maintainers, SCA vendors — all currently drowning in fake-CVE noise, all with budget.

### Direction 2 — Install-time lifecycle script firewall (attack surface now proven)

**What to build:** the technical entry point for Shai-Hulud was mundane — one `"preinstall": "node setup.mjs"` line, executed automatically before `npm install` finishes. Build a guard for CI and local dev that blocks install-time lifecycle scripts by default, executes them in a sandbox while recording what they actually do (outbound network, `~/.ssh` access, environment reads), and requires human confirmation for scripts that are new or recently changed. **The pitch is one sentence: prove your CI didn’t exfiltrate anything during those 30 seconds of `npm install`.**

**Stack:** `--ignore-scripts` as the baseline with an allowlist for packages that genuinely compile; bubblewrap or microVMs for the sandbox; eBPF or a DNS interception layer for egress monitoring; ship it as a GitHub Action for distribution. All logic is deterministic — **no LLM calls, so marginal cost is near zero.**

**Cost and timeline:** 4–6 weeks to usable. Watch the monetization clock: incident-driven demand decays fast. **Convert panic into compliance within two to three months** — position as continuous auditing, not a one-time sweep, or renewals evaporate with the news cycle.

### Direction 3 — Vendor-neutral AI spend metering (the supply side is actively deleting this data)

**What to build:** the significance of Cursor’s change isn’t Cursor — it’s the structural incentive it reveals. **Tool vendors have no reason to help you see what each call costs.** So the data has to be collected outside the vendor. Build a cross-harness real-time cost meter covering Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Pi, reporting true dollar spend per session, per task, per model, plus same-task A/B comparisons across harnesses. People on HN are already hand-rolling this — that’s both competition and demand validation.

**Stack:** a local proxy intercepting API traffic (the only collection point that routes around vendor UIs); DuckDB for traces; a minimal panel. **Key differentiator: don’t report token counts alone, report cost per completed task.** As one HN commenter correctly noted, fewer tokens isn’t automatically better — the agent may have skipped checks it should have run. Present quality alongside cost or you’re incentivizing benchmark gaming.

**Cost and timeline:** near-zero infrastructure; 4–6 weeks. Comparable observability tools sit in the $29–99/seat/month band. State the risk plainly: vendors may tighten proxy access, so plan for being targeted.

### Direction 4 — Managed maintenance for personalized forks (productizing Crawshaw’s two prompts)

**What to build:** turn “nightly fetch upstream, rebase local patches, verify, replace” into a service. A user declares “I use this open source tool and I want these three modifications,” and the system maintains a private fork continuously — auto-rebasing on upstream releases, running tests, surfacing conflict resolutions. **This converts a prompt into an SLA.** An individual can self-host it; but once five people on a team depend on the customized build, nobody wants to be the person the 3 a.m. cron wakes up.

**Stack:** GitHub App for repo integration; isolated containers for build verification; a coding agent for rebase conflicts — one of the few genuinely reliable agent scenarios, because there’s an unambiguous correctness oracle: the tests either pass or they don’t; plus change-summary notifications. Pick one or two high-customization tools (editors, terminals, agent frameworks themselves) to build credibility.

**Cost and timeline:** $30–100/month build compute; 8–10 weeks. Natural pricing is per maintained fork, and retention should be strong — once a team depends on a customized build, switching cost is brutal.

### Direction 5 — Encode your own expert judgment (slowest, most depreciation-resistant)

**What to build:** if “LLMs amplify expertise” holds, then **the tradable asset isn’t access to a model, it’s knowing what correct output looks like.** Pick one narrow domain where you have a decade of real judgment and encode your acceptance criteria — not a general agent that generates, but an **expert review layer that judges whether generated output passes.** Crawshaw’s `meat` is the minimal instance: he encoded twenty years of “which diff lines aren’t worth a human’s attention” into a tool.

**Stack:** deterministic rule checks as the foundation (do **not** use an LLM here — explainability *is* the trust proposition), a small model for classification (Haiku tier suffices), your own labeled data for calibration. **The moat is the labeled data and the criteria, not the model** — so model upgrades reduce your operating cost instead of eroding your position.

**Cost and timeline:** 3–6 months to revenue, the slowest of the five. But it’s the only one where a 20% better model makes it *more* valuable rather than less — a counterfactual test worth running against every line of your own roadmap.

## Risk Factors

- **Using AI to verify AI is a dead end, and the easiest trap in this space.** The fake CVEs beat NVD precisely because they were textually flawless. A verification product whose core is “have a model judge authenticity” will fail in the same place the same way. **Value must come from deterministic execution — compiling, running, symbol existence — with the LLM confined to the edges.**
- **Incident-driven demand decays fast.** Everyone is sharing the npm story now; in six weeks nobody will remember. Too many security tools get a signup spike after an incident and single-digit renewal rates three months later. Convert panic into process or you’ve built consulting, not a product.
- **“Experts won’t be replaced” is a comfortable conclusion, and comfort warrants a discount.** Goedecke himself notes commenters who find it suspiciously reassuring, and concedes that by the time anyone studies it properly the landscape will have shifted again. Treat it as a valid tactical read for now, not as long-term identity insurance.
- **Observability products built on vendor APIs can be cut off.** Cursor has already demonstrated willingness to remove cost data, and one user noted the inability to proxy API requests. If your collection channel depends on someone else’s goodwill, that’s not architecture — it’s a lease.
- **Personalized forks accrue a new species of technical debt.** Crawshaw’s approach is elegant, but with a dozen auto-rebasing private forks, one upstream breaking refactor eventually yields a merge that **passes tests while being semantically wrong.** Automation made maintenance cheap, not free.

## Action Recommendations

### If you’re early (no stable revenue yet)

- ✅ **Audit your own dependency tree this week, deterministically.** Check whether you pulled an affected version, and flip `--ignore-scripts` on in CI to see what breaks. That exercise is free market research for Direction 2.
- ✅ **Don’t build a pure generation product.** Two independent events this week showed the marginal value of generated output collapsing. The same effort spent on verification, auditing, and proof faces the opposite curve.
- ✅ **Honestly inventory where you have a decade of judgment.** If the answer is nowhere, your first priority isn’t finding a project — it’s going deep in one narrow domain. In this new division of labor, **the person without expertise is the meat proxy.**
- ✅ **Run one week of “the agent doesn’t touch files.”** Make it propose in chat; type it in yourself. You’ll immediately discover how much code you’ve been merging without understanding — more instructive than any methodology essay.

### If you’re running a product with revenue

- ✅ **Treat install-time scripts as production surface.** That `npm install` in your CI has full network and filesystem access. Two billion monthly installs this week proved it isn’t theoretical.
- ✅ **Start collecting cost data into your own systems now.** Don’t depend on any vendor’s usage page — it can vanish on a Tuesday morning without an announcement.
- ✅ **Run the counterfactual on every roadmap item: if the underlying model is 20% better tomorrow, does this get more valuable or less?** Double down on the former, start thinning the latter. That test would have flagged every loser in this week’s news a year early.
- ✅ **If you depend on a closed-source dev tool, price out the fork.** Not to migrate today — just to know the number. The Cursor lesson isn’t that they did something uniquely wrong; it’s that **you have no leverage over a tool you can’t fork.**

## What it means

The three stories describe one movement. Fake CVEs beat the vulnerability disclosure system because that system used polish as a proxy for effort. The npm worm beat the supply chain because provenance proves origin, not intent. And the two posts developers argued about most were both, underneath, about the same anxiety: when the machine produces the artifact, what exactly is the human contributing?

The answer this week converged from three directions: **the human contributes verification, and verification requires expertise.** Tao gets different output from the same model because he can tell what’s wrong with it. JFrog caught the fakes because researchers who know SQLite internals noticed a function that shouldn’t exist. The reviewer who reads every line is doing the work the meat proxy skipped.

If you’re deciding what to build this month, the filter is unchanged from the reproduction test itself: **can a machine cheaply fake the output of your product?** If yes, your margins are on a countdown. If your product’s whole job is to *detect* that fakery — by compiling, running, and checking rather than by reading — you’re on the side of this trade where supply just vanished and demand just arrived. Everything polished is now suspect, and being the one who checks is a business.
