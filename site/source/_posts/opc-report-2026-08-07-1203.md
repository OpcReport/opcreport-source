# Same Model, 22% or 37% Tool-Calling Accuracy Depending on Who Serves It

**August 7, 2026**

You picked a model. You benchmarked it, maybe, or you read someone else’s benchmark. Then you routed production traffic to whichever provider was cheapest per token, because the weights are the weights.

That assumption just got measured, and it does not hold.

Artificial Analysis launched an **Endpoint Accuracy Index** this week: they self-host the official weights at the lab’s recommended precision, following the lab’s own serving recipe, and then score every serverless endpoint as a percentage of that reference. Initial coverage is GLM-5.2, gpt-oss-120b, and DeepSeek V4 Pro.

The headline result on **gpt-oss-120b**: providers parse and format tool calls differently, and **some endpoints score 22% on BFCL-500 against 37% for the reference.** For an agent product, tool-calling accuracy is not a nice-to-have benchmark line — it is the product. You are running the model at roughly 60% of its actual capability and your invoice says nothing about it.

On **GLM-5.2**, the failure mode is different and dumber: restrictive output-token limits cut responses off before the model finishes reasoning, and the most restrictive endpoints score **half the reference or less** on scientific reasoning. The general pattern across both models: endpoints scoring below reference produce **roughly half the reference’s output tokens**. They look cheap because they are quietly doing less work.

The good news, such as it is: **DeepSeek V4 Pro endpoints mostly sit at reference parity**, and DeepSeek’s own first-party endpoint scores slightly *above* the reference. Provider variance is not universal. It is just unmeasured.

Put this next to the cost spread everyone has been discussing all week and the picture inverts. The debate was *which model is worth the price*. The actual live question is **whether the endpoint you are paying for is running the model you think you bought.**

## Key Insights

- **Provider choice is now a silent accuracy variable.** Same open weights, same model name, 22% vs 37% on tool calling (BFCL-500) for gpt-oss-120b. Endpoints below reference generally emit about half the reference’s output tokens — the discount is coming out of capability, not efficiency.
- **The measurement method is copyable and it is the interesting part.** Three equally-weighted areas: tool calling (BFCL-500, 500 questions × 3 repeats), scientific reasoning (HLE-250, 250 × 10), long-context recall (AA-LCR-25, 25 × 10), each scored against a self-hosted reference, with parity defined by overlapping 95% confidence intervals. You can run a cheap version of this against your own workload.
- **Model version bumps raise your bill even when the price sheet doesn’t move.** Meta’s Muse Spark 1.2 kept 1.1’s pricing exactly ($1.25/$4.25 per 1M, cache hits $0.15) and still went from **$0.29 to $0.40 per benchmark task** — input tokens up ~53%, output up ~36%. Qwen3.8 Max takes **#1 on the agentic index** at #9 intelligence (58) for $2/$6 with an 88%-off cache hit at $0.25. ## Market Trends

### 1. The serving layer became a quality decision, and nobody has been auditing it

For the last two years, “which model” was treated as the whole decision and “which provider” as a procurement detail — a spreadsheet exercise about dollars per million tokens and tokens per second. The Endpoint Accuracy Index is the first systematic evidence that this framing is wrong.

Three distinct mechanisms show up in the data, and they fail differently:

- **Tool-call parsing and formatting.** Providers implement this themselves, and implementations disagree. gpt-oss-120b: 22% on some endpoints, 37% on the reference. This is the one that matters most for agents, because a mis-parsed tool call is not a slightly worse answer — it is a broken step in a chain.
- **Output-token ceilings.** Restrictive limits truncate the model mid-reasoning. On GLM-5.2, the most restrictive endpoints land at half the reference or below on HLE-250. The model didn’t get dumber; it got interrupted.
- **Serving configuration drift.** Some endpoints produce far fewer reasoning tokens *at the same requested settings*, and restricted context windows quietly break long-context tasks.

The tell that ties them together: low-scoring endpoints emit roughly half the reference’s output tokens. Cheapness that comes from doing less thinking is not cheapness, it is a downgrade billed as a bargain.

Worth noting what the index does *not* say: DeepSeek V4 Pro endpoints are mostly at parity, with the first-party endpoint slightly above reference. So this isn’t “third-party providers bad.” It’s that the variance exists, it is large, it is model-specific, and until this week nobody was publishing it.

**What this means for you:** if you route through an aggregator or picked a provider on price, you have an unmeasured input to your product’s quality. Fix the measurement before you fix the routing — you may find your current provider is fine, and you may find you’ve been shipping at 60%.

### 2. Version upgrades are a cost event, and the price sheet won’t warn you

The Muse Spark 1.2 numbers are the cleanest illustration of a trap that is going to catch a lot of small operators this year.

Meta kept pricing **identical** to 1.1: $1.25 per 1M input, $4.25 per 1M output, cache hits at $0.15, 1M-token context. Capability improved meaningfully — GDPval-AA v2 Elo jumped 260 points to 1631 (fifth overall, ahead of Claude Opus 4.8), Terminal-Bench v2.1 went 78%→80%, τ³-Banking 25%→27%.

And cost per benchmark task went from **$0.29 to $0.40**. A 38% increase with zero price change, because input tokens rose ~53% and output ~36%.

If you auto-upgrade to the newest point release of your model — which most people do, because staying current is the default and the marketing says “better” — your unit economics move without any announcement you could have set an alert on. **Verbosity is the pricing lever nobody publishes.**

There’s a second-order version of the same problem in the benchmark layer itself. Intelligence Index v4.1.1 landed this week: τ³-Banking updated to Sierra’s v1.0.1, and HLE, AA-LCR and AA-Omniscience graders unified onto GPT-5.6 Luna (medium), replacing a mix of GPT-4o, Qwen3 235B, and Gemini 3 Flash Preview. Rankings held — Opus 5 still #1 at 63 — but scores drifted upward and Muse Spark 1.2 gained 2.7 points from the grader change alone.

**What this means for you:** track cost per completed task, not price per million tokens. And if you put benchmark numbers in a sales deck, version-stamp them, because the number can move without the model moving.

### 3. The free tier is now good enough to delete a product category

OpenAI removed text chat limits for every ChatGPT user this week. Free and Go users get GPT-5.6 Luna as default plus a “Think” button for harder questions; Plus and Pro get an upgraded Sol and a thinking slider. Internal evaluation: factual errors **62% less common for Luna, 68% for Sol** against GPT-5.5-Instant. The install base is **1 billion weekly users**.

For anyone whose product is a prompt, a system message, and a payment form, that’s the whole story. Unlimited, free, fewer errors, already installed.

What survives is what has never been in the box: proprietary or hard-to-collect data, workflow integration into systems the assistant can’t reach, accountability someone will pay for, and domain judgment. The same week produced an unusually good articulation of this from a developer whose essay hit the HN front page — the argument that when the cost of producing an artifact collapses, the filter that used to be *effort* has to be replaced by *judgment*, and judgment is built by living with your own failures rather than by consuming good work.

That post also produced its own object lesson, which is the more actionable half. The author appended a post-mortem after commenters accused the writing of reading like AI slop. **In 2026 the accusation is now a default suspicion, and it attaches to good work too.** If your distribution runs through written content, “clearly a human made this and knows something” is a positioning problem you have to actively solve — specificity, first-hand numbers, named mistakes, things a model has no way to have.

### 4. The exit for a vertical agent product is an incumbent’s customer list

Klaviyo acquired Agency this week. The specifics are the useful part: Agency was three years old, had raised **$32M** from Sequoia, Menlo Ventures and Felicis, ran a **25-person team**, and built AI for customer success. Founder Elias Torres joins Klaviyo as CPO to lead its agent products — Composer for campaign building and Customer Agent for post-sale support — with the stated aim of getting them to Klaviyo’s **200,000 businesses**.

Torres has done this before: Performable to HubSpot in 2011, Drift to Vista at a $1.2B valuation.

Read the trade honestly. The incumbent had distribution and needed agent capability. The startup had agent capability and would have spent years buying distribution. Both sides made a rational trade, and the rational trade keeps being available because agent capability is now buildable by small teams while 200,000 customer relationships are not.

The same logic is showing up in adjacent shapes. MacPaw is wiring Liquid AI’s models into its products and preparing to hand that local-inference stack — plus its SetApp store — to third-party developers building AI apps. Ex-Spotify engineers raised **$10M** to bring music-recommendation-style behavioral modeling to e-commerce. Omilia raised **$67M** for customer support. Naïve raised **$28.5M** packaging the whole company-formation stack behind an API. Every one of these is a layer being commoditized and sold to people who don’t want to build it.

**What this means for you:** if you’re building a vertical agent, build it to be bought. Clean data boundaries, a portable eval suite, a team small enough to acquihire, and depth in one industry. That is a better-priced outcome than trying to out-distribute an incumbent that already has the customers.

## Build Directions

### 1. Endpoint accuracy auditing for agent products

**What to build:** the Endpoint Accuracy Index covers three open-weights models. Nobody is doing it for *your* workload, and your workload is the only one you actually care about. Build the harness that pins a golden task set from a customer’s real traffic, runs it against every endpoint they could route to, and reports accuracy-per-dollar rather than tokens-per-dollar.

Sell it two ways: as a one-off audit (“you are running at 60% of reference on tool calls, here’s the endpoint to move to”) and as continuous monitoring, because providers change serving configuration without telling anyone.

**Stack:** self-host reference weights on a rented H100 hour or use the lab’s first-party endpoint as your baseline; BFCL-style tool-calling cases drawn from the customer’s own tool schemas; 3–10 repeats per case for tight intervals; Postgres for history; a diff report when an endpoint drifts. Keep the eval set small and repeat it heavily — that’s the trick that makes it cheap.

**Cost/timeline:** 3–5 weeks to a sellable audit. Reference runs are the main line item and they’re hours, not months. Pricing: $2–5k per audit, $200–500/month for monitoring. The distribution is free — publish your provider scorecard.

### 2. Cost-per-completed-task instrumentation

**What to build:** everyone measures tokens. Almost nobody measures **dollars per successfully completed task**, which is the only number that survives a version bump. The Muse Spark 1.2 case — 38% cost increase at unchanged prices — is invisible to token dashboards and obvious to task-cost dashboards.

Ship a wrapper that tags every model call with a task ID and an outcome, then reports cost per success, cost per retry, and cost drift after any model or provider change, with alerts on regression.

**Stack:** OpenTelemetry-style tracing around your model client, task/outcome tagging, a cheap model for automated outcome classification where success isn’t structurally detectable, a weekly digest. Ship it as an open-source SDK plus a hosted dashboard — the SDK is your distribution.

**Cost/timeline:** 2–4 weeks for a usable version. This is also a category where you should dogfood first and sell second; your own numbers are the case study.

### 3. Local-first AI apps through curated store channels

**What to build:** MacPaw is bundling Liquid AI local inference and opening it to third-party SetApp developers. That’s a distribution channel with a built-in inference runtime and an audience that already pays a subscription. Marginal inference cost near zero changes what’s economically viable — background processing, continuous monitoring, always-on assistants that would be absurd at API prices.

Pick a workload that is high-volume, latency-sensitive, and privacy-adjacent: local document triage, transcription and summarization, code or file organization, personal knowledge indexing.

**Stack:** Liquid AI or comparable small models via the platform runtime, native Swift/Electron shell, local vector storage, cloud escalation only for genuinely hard queries. Design the escalation boundary deliberately — that’s where your margin lives.

**Cost/timeline:** 6–10 weeks. Platform risk is real and you should price it in, but curated-store distribution is worth more than most indie developers’ entire marketing budget.

### 4. Vertical agents built for acquisition

**What to build:** Klaviyo/Agency is the template. Pick an industry where an incumbent SaaS platform owns the customer relationship and is visibly behind on agents. Build the agent layer that platform will eventually need — deep in workflow, clean at the data boundary, with an eval suite that proves quality to a skeptical acquirer.

The evals are not a formality. They are the artifact that makes diligence fast and makes your capability legible to someone who can’t evaluate your code.

**Stack:** Claude Code or Codex to build, the platform’s public API as your integration surface, Postgres for state, a documented eval suite as a first-class deliverable. Keep the team small; a 25-person team is acquirable, a 60-person team is a merger.

**Cost/timeline:** 6–18 months to a credible position. This is the slowest direction on this list and the highest-ceiling one, and it only works if you genuinely know the industry.

### 5. Provenance and specificity tooling for content-led distribution

**What to build:** the AI-slop accusation is now the default reader prior, and it lands on real work. Meanwhile Shopify’s Q2 data showed AI-driven traffic and orders tripling year over year, concentrated in small merchants — so content distribution matters *more*, not less, and it now has to survive a credibility filter it didn’t have two years ago.

Build the tooling that makes human-specific work legible: first-hand data extraction from a client’s own systems into publishable numbers, source-and-timestamp attachment, structured data for assistant citation, and a check for the generic-phrasing patterns that trigger the slop reflex.

**Stack:** schema.org structured data as the machine-readable output, a scheduled assistant-mention monitor (does ChatGPT/Perplexity/Gemini name this client, and how), the client’s own analytics or ops data as the specificity source, a weekly report. Cheap models on off-peak batching — this workload has no latency requirement.

**Cost/timeline:** 2–4 weeks to MVP, $99–299/month, buyer is any small business doing content marketing. Low ceiling, fast revenue, good first product.

## Risk Factors

- **Your provider can change serving configuration silently.** Output limits, reasoning-token budgets, context windows and tool-call parsing are all provider-side decisions that move without notice. If accuracy matters to your product, you need a canary eval running on a schedule, not a one-time provider decision.
- **Auto-upgrading models is a live cost risk.** Muse Spark 1.2 cost 38% more per task than 1.1 at identical published prices. Pin versions, measure the delta yourself, then upgrade on purpose.
- **The free tier is a competitor with a billion weekly users and no marginal cost.** Unlimited free text chat plus a 62–68% reduction in factual errors sets a floor. If you can’t articulate what you have that the free tier structurally cannot get — data, integration, accountability, judgment — you don’t have a business, you have a markup.
- **Leaderboard numbers move without models moving.** Index v4.1.1 changed graders and shifted scores by up to 2.7 points. Version-stamp any benchmark claim you make to a customer, or you will eventually be caught quoting a number that no longer exists.
- **Cheap endpoints can be expensive.** An endpoint at half the reference’s accuracy costs you retries, failed agent runs, and support load. Compare on cost per *completed* task or you are optimizing the wrong variable with high confidence.
- **The slop prior is now attached to good work.** Even careful, original writing gets accused. Budget for specificity — named numbers, first-hand mistakes, things only you would know — as a functional requirement of distribution, not as style.
- **Acquisition is an outcome, not a plan.** Build for it structurally, but the version of this that works is a real business with real customers that an incumbent wants. Nobody buys a demo.

## Action Recommendations

**If you’re early or solo:**

- ✅ Run a 50-case tool-calling eval against your current endpoint this week, and against one alternative. Two afternoons, and it’s the highest-information experiment available to you right now.
- ✅ Pin your model version explicitly instead of tracking “latest.” Then measure cost per completed task before and after every upgrade you choose to take.
- ✅ Write down, in one sentence, what your product has that unlimited free ChatGPT structurally cannot get. If the sentence is hard to write, change the product, not the copy.
- ✅ If your model spend is meaningful, check whether your provider has peak/off-peak pricing and whether your batchable work can move off-peak. Free money for a config change.
- ✅ If you distribute through writing, put at least one number in every piece that could only have come from your own systems. That’s the cheapest available defense against the slop prior.

**If you’re running something established:**

- ✅ Stand up a scheduled canary eval — a small fixed task set, run daily against production endpoints, alerting on accuracy drift. Providers change things; you should find out from your dashboard, not your customers.
- ✅ Audit your actual routing: which model, which provider, which output-token limit, which context window. Most teams cannot answer all four from memory, and all four affect accuracy.
- ✅ Convert your cost reporting from tokens to cost per completed task, per feature. It’s the only view where a verbosity increase shows up as what it is.
- ✅ Build your eval suite as a portable, documented artifact. It’s your quality control today and the single most useful thing you can hand an acquirer or an enterprise buyer later.
- ✅ Re-examine whether some workload belongs on-device. If the marginal cost of inference goes to zero, features you rejected as uneconomic may become your differentiator.

## What It Means

The last three months of this conversation have been about picking the right model. That question is close to solved: there’s a public leaderboard, there’s a Pareto curve, and the answer is usually “a cheaper one than you’re using, for most of your calls.”

The question that replaced it is quieter and worse. **The model you selected is not necessarily the model that runs.** The endpoint changes it. The output limit truncates it. The version bump inflates it. And none of that appears anywhere in your invoice or your provider’s status page.

So both questions this report exists to answer point the same direction this week. *Which direction to build:* toward measurement — endpoint audits, task-cost instrumentation, portable eval suites, the boring infrastructure of knowing what you’re actually running. *Which tools:* your own eval harness first, then whatever routing follows from what it tells you.

The free tier took the bottom of the market. The incumbents are buying the middle. What’s left for one person is the part that requires someone to have actually checked — and right now, almost nobody has.
