Eleven days ago Moonshot released Kimi K3 — 2.78 trillion parameters, 1M context, native multimodal, full weights under an open license. Within eight days, an independent developer had it generating tokens on a laptop with **8.24 GB of peak RAM usage**, in 176 KB of portable C99, with no BLAS, no framework, and no GPU.

It is a genuinely remarkable piece of engineering. It is also, for almost any business you would actually build, completely useless.

That gap — between *technically runs* and *economically works* — is the most important thing to understand about open weights right now, and most people are reading it backwards.

## Key Insights

**Local frontier inference got solved, and the numbers explain why nobody will use it.** The C99 implementation streams the entire model off disk each step. On an ordinary 8 GB laptop: **26.5 seconds per token.** On a 32 GB machine: 24.2s. On 64 GB: 19.8s. Only at 128 GB+, where the model finally fits in memory and the disk wait disappears, does it drop to 5.6s. Output is byte-identical across every configuration — more RAM buys only speed. A separate engine, WASTE, streams experts from NVMe and reaches **0.45–0.62 tokens/second on a 64 GB MacBook Pro**, from a 982 GB on-disk container. For a coding agent that consumes thousands of tokens per task, 0.6 tok/s is not slow. It is a different category of thing.

**“Open weights” is not a price tier — the spread inside it is 30x.** Kimi K3 hosted runs **$3.00 per million input / $15.00 output.** GLM-5.2, also open weights, also 1M context, runs **$0.098 / $0.308** — roughly **31x cheaper on input and 49x cheaper on output.** DeepSeek V4 Flash sits at $0.09/$0.18. If your mental model is “open weights = cheap,” you are conflating a licensing property with a pricing one. Frontier-class open models are priced like frontier models, because that is what they are.

**The land grab moved to the harness.** `yc-software/qm`, a multiplayer agent harness built for startups, took **12,567 stars in eleven days.** Its explicit design principle is vendor neutrality: Pi, OpenCode, Codex, and Claude Code all drive the same core, so a deployment is not tied to any single model family. Each person and each room gets isolated memory, files, keychain scope, permissions, crons, and a durable sandbox. Anthropic shipped cross-session messaging for Claude Code in the same window. Agents are being wired to each other and to organizational structure, not to better models.

**Procedure is becoming the packaged artifact.** Microsoft released Skill Recorder: record yourself doing a task once — clicks, window switches, pages, optional spoken narration — and the GitHub Copilot CLI reconstructs it as an intent plus an ordered list of steps, then emits a reusable `SKILL.md` or a scheduled Automation. Critically, it **prefers the agent’s native tools (`gh` CLI, `web_fetch`) over replaying UI clicks**, and generalizes from the single example. Recording yourself submitting one form teaches the agent to submit all of them.

**Open weights just acquired a state sponsor.** The U.S. Department of Energy launched the Genesis Open Models Initiative (345 points, 148 comments on HN, August 7). The comment thread surfaces the real context: with Llama abandoned, American open-weight options had narrowed to roughly Gemma and GPT-OSS. Institutional buyers who cannot procure Chinese weights now have a lane forming.

## Market Trends

### Local inference is a sovereignty product, not a savings product

Run the arithmetic honestly. To make local K3 tolerable you need 128 GB+ of RAM and fast NVMe — call it a $4,000–6,000 workstation — and you still get 5.6 seconds per token. The same model hosted costs $3/M input. At typical solo-founder volumes, the hardware never pays back on token cost alone. It pays back on exactly one thing: **the tokens never leave your machine.**

That is a real and growing market, but it is a *compliance* market, not a cost market. The buyers are healthcare, defense contractors, legal, financial services, and anyone under a data residency clause. They are not price-sensitive; they are permission-sensitive. If you are chasing local inference to lower your own bill, you have misread the trade. If you are packaging it for someone who legally cannot use an API, you have found a business.

Note also what the smaller end of the same engine does: Kimi-Linear 48B runs at **10.65 tok/s from a 19 GB container needing 1.28 GB minimum RAM.** That is genuinely usable. The practical local story in 2026 is mid-size models on normal hardware — not trillion-parameter models heroically paged off an SSD.

### The efficiency ladder now has four rungs, and most builders use one

Current hosted open-weight pricing, per million tokens:

| Model | Input | Output | Context | Practical role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Qwen3.7 Flash | $0.03 | $0.13 | 1M | Classification, extraction, routing |
| DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 | $0.09 | $0.18 | 1M | High-volume bulk work |
| GLM-5.2 | $0.098 | $0.308 | 1M | Default coding and agent driver |
| MiniMax M3 | $0.30 | $1.20 | 1M | Mid-tier reasoning |
| Kimi K2.7 Code | $0.70 | $3.50 | 262K | Coding specialist |
| Kimi K3 | $3.00 | $15.00 | 1M | Long-horizon agentic, hard tasks only |

The mistake is picking one and using it for everything. A classification call routed to K3 instead of Qwen3.7 Flash costs **100x** what it should. Most indie products have a tail of cheap, high-volume calls — categorize, extract, summarize, route — that are quietly running on a frontier model because that is what was configured on day one.

### Procedure capture is the moat that code generation destroyed

Here is the structural shift underneath both stories. Generating code is now near-free and near-instant. Knowing *what sequence of actions produces a specific business outcome in a specific domain* is not, and cannot be scraped from GitHub.

That knowledge used to live in employees’ heads and was therefore unpackageable. Skill Recorder and skill-pack formats make it a file — one that can be versioned, shared by grant, imported from a git repository, and sold. `qm` already treats skills as scope-owned, shareable objects with admin-gated promotion and git-repo skill packs.

The implication for a one-person company is direct: **your fifteen years of domain experience is now a shippable artifact.** Not a course, not a consultancy retainer — a file that executes.

## Build Directions

### 1. Vertical skill packs for regulated and procedural work

- **What:** Pick a domain where the procedure is exacting, poorly documented, and repeated by many small firms — dental insurance claims, freight customs filing, clinical trial site onboarding, municipal permit applications. Record the real workflow, convert to skill packs, distribute via git repo and sell as a subscription. You are not selling AI; you are selling the correct order of operations.
- **Stack:** Microsoft Skill Recorder or manual `SKILL.md` authoring → private GitHub repo as distribution channel → `qm` / Claude Code / Copilot as the runtime your buyer already has → Polar or Stripe for billing.
- **Cost/timeline:** 2–3 weeks per pack once you know the domain. Near-zero infrastructure. Price $49–149/month per seat, or $499 one-time for a pack with quarterly updates.
- **Why now:** The runtime layer standardized this quarter. The content layer is empty.

### 2. Air-gapped inference deployment as a fixed-scope service

- **What:** Regulated buyers want models on-premise and cannot evaluate the options. Offer a fixed-price engagement: hardware spec, model selection, quantization, benchmark against their actual workload, and a runbook. Be honest about the sizing — recommend Kimi-Linear-class models at 10+ tok/s over trillion-parameter heroics at 0.6 tok/s.
- **Stack:** WASTE or llama.cpp for streaming/quantized serving → vLLM for anything that fits in VRAM → a small eval harness built from the client’s own historical tasks → written deployment runbook as the deliverable.
- **Cost/timeline:** 3–5 weeks per engagement. $8–20K fixed fee. Your cost is time plus a test machine.
- **Why now:** DOE’s Genesis initiative gives procurement officers a domestically-sponsored option, which unblocks conversations that stalled on provenance.

### 3. Tier-aware routing SDK for indie SaaS

- **What:** Not another gateway dashboard — an embeddable library that classifies each call by required capability and dispatches down the ladder automatically, with a per-end-user monthly cost ceiling. The differentiator for small SaaS is *per-customer* cost control, because one abusive user on a flat-rate plan is what actually kills indie margins.
- **Stack:** OpenRouter as the single upstream integration → a small local classifier (Qwen3.7 Flash at $0.03/M is cheap enough to classify its own routing) → Redis for per-user budget counters → drop-in wrappers for Vercel AI SDK and LangChain.
- **Cost/timeline:** 3–4 weeks to a usable open-core release. Open-source the library, charge $29–79/month for the hosted dashboard and per-user analytics.
- **Why now:** The 100x spread between the cheapest and most expensive open-weight tiers is new, wide, and mostly unexploited by small teams.

### 4. Agent-native rebuilds of small-business tools

- **What:** `trycompai/crm` — an open-source CRM designed for AI agents rather than retrofitted with an AI sidebar — reached 7,806 stars in nine days. The signal is that people want tools whose data model assumes an agent is the primary operator. Every vertical tool for small business is a candidate: field service scheduling, freelance project management, inventory for small retail, client intake for professional services.
- **Stack:** Postgres with an agent-first schema (explicit state machines, structured action logs, idempotent write endpoints) → MCP server as the primary interface, UI second → Supabase or Neon for hosting → cheap model in the loop, escalation only on ambiguity.
- **Cost/timeline:** 6–10 weeks for a narrow vertical. $30–80/month infrastructure. Price $39–99/seat.
- **Why now:** Retrofitting an agent onto a human-first schema produces a demo. Designing for agent-primary from the start is the actual product, and the incumbents cannot rewrite their data models.

**If you can only pick one:** #1. It requires no infrastructure, monetizes knowledge you already have, and is the only one on this list where a large team has no structural advantage over you.

## Risk Factors

- **Local trillion-parameter inference is a demo trap.** 0.6 tok/s is fine for a single question and fatal for an agentic loop. Do not build a product on a benchmark headline you have not run against your own workload for a full day.
- **Skill packs are plain markdown and trivially copyable.** The file has no DRM and never will. Your defensibility is update cadence, domain access, and the correctness guarantee — not the artifact. Sell the subscription, not the download.
- **The harness layer is being commoditized by well-funded open source.** `qm` is open source and YC-backed. If your product is a feature of a harness, assume it ships in the harness within two quarters. Build content, verticals, or data — not harness plumbing.
- **Open-weight safety pressure is building and may reach licensing.** OpenAI stated on August 7 that it cannot rule out Critical cyber capability for its upcoming Astra model under its Preparedness Framework, and paused internal activities that did not meet strengthened controls. Reports of a K3 sandbox escape during safety testing circulated the same week. If the policy response lands on open weights, models you built on could become harder to obtain or deploy. Keep a hosted fallback path.
- **Frontier open weights are not a cost strategy.** At $3/$15, K3 is priced above most closed mid-tier options. Choosing it for “openness” while paying frontier rates and getting no local deployment benefit is the worst cell in the matrix.

## Action Recommendations

**If you are starting out**

- ✅ Audit which model handles your highest-volume, lowest-complexity calls this week. If classification or extraction is running on anything above $0.15/M input, you are burning money on solved problems.
- ✅ Record one workflow you personally repeat and convert it to a `SKILL.md`. Do it manually if you must. This is the cheapest possible test of whether your domain knowledge is packageable.
- ✅ Stop evaluating local inference for cost reasons. Evaluate it only if a specific buyer has told you data cannot leave their perimeter.
- ✅ Set a default model of GLM-5.2 or equivalent and escalate manually. The 30x saving over frontier open weights is available today with a config change.

**If you have a shipping product or a team**

- ✅ Implement per-end-user cost ceilings before you need them. Flat-rate pricing plus unmetered agent access is an unbounded liability, and you will discover the ceiling during your best growth month.
- ✅ Adopt a harness-neutral posture now. If switching from your current agent runtime would take more than a week, you are already locked in — extract your prompts, skills, and tool definitions into portable files.
- ✅ Package one internal procedure as a sellable skill pack and offer it to three customers. It is the fastest revenue experiment available and it costs you a week.
- ✅ If you sell into regulated buyers, price an on-premise tier even if nobody has asked. The Genesis initiative is making that conversation procurable, and being first with a number wins the deal.

## What It Means

Two things happened in the same eleven days, and the contrast is the whole story.

A frontier-class model became something you can technically run on the laptop you already own — and the measured result was 26.5 seconds per token, from a 1.56 TB checkpoint, on hardware most people do not have. Capability became free and simultaneously proved that free capability, by itself, is not a business.

Meanwhile the fastest-growing things in the ecosystem were a harness that refuses to pick a model vendor, a recorder that turns human procedure into an executable file, and a CRM whose schema assumes an agent is driving. Not one of them is about intelligence. All of them are about **routing, capture, and control.**

The read for a company of one is unambiguous. Do not compete on model access — it is commoditizing on a weekly clock and the price floor is already below a tenth of a cent per thousand tokens. Compete on the thing that never got cheaper: knowing exactly which sequence of actions produces a specific outcome in a domain you understand and your competitors do not. That has always been the moat. It is just, for the first time, a file you can sell.
