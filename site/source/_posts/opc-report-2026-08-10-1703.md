# Rippling Was Burning 40% of Its R&D Payroll on Tokens — Then Shipped the Cure as a Product

**August 10, 2026**

---

## Key Insights

The most informative thing published this week wasn’t a model release. It was an invoice.

Rippling went all-in on tokenmaxxing at the start of the year. By March, its CFO put a number in front of the executive team that stopped the room: the company was **on track to spend 40% of its R&D headcount budget on AI tokens** — as much on inference as it paid 40% of the people in that org. Spend was growing **80% month over month**. Extrapolated a year out, tokens would have cost 90% of what R&D payroll cost.

The first move was not a cap. It was an audit. The finding: **10–15% of employees drove roughly 60% of total AI spend, and one engineer was spending $50,000 a month.** Then they built the fix, and then they sold it — AI Spend Console shipped this week as a product.

The result worth memorizing: **July usage returned to the 600-billion-token peak, but July’s cost was 37% of April’s cost.** Usage never came down. Only routing changed.

That reframes the second half of 2026. Model capability is no longer the pitch. The bill is. What buyers now pay to solve is: how much did this cost, who spent it, and was it worth it.

**Signals that should change what you do this week:**

- **Same usage, roughly two-thirds of the cost.** Rippling cut token spend from 40% of R&D payroll to about 15% by building an internal AI gateway and negotiating a hard cap with each vendor — Cursor, OpenAI, Anthropic. Reference point from CEO Parker Conrad’s internal benchmarks: **GLM 5.2 came in 85% cheaper with nearly identical performance** to frontier models.
- **“Starting a company” is being reduced to an API call.** YC-backed Naïve raised a **$28.5M Series A** (Nexus Venture Partners leading, ~$32M total), packaging US LLC formation, email, phone numbers, virtual cards, databases, compute, and Stripe/QuickBooks behind one API that Cursor, Claude Code, or Codex can drive. **Over 30,000 developer customers within months of launch**, ARR up 10x in six months to low double-digit millions — with **10 full-time employees.** Customers are running faceless content channels, AI automation agencies, and an autonomous rental car agency.
- **Capability is abundant; the experience is still broken.** Engineer Kira Howe’s post “Is it all just vapourware?” hit the HN front page with 181 comments. She tried an agentic dev platform, couldn’t log in on desktop at all, got in via web, connected a project — and **burned her entire $20 of compute credits thrashing on the step of fetching a todo list out of Linear.** Zero code written. Her framing is the sharp part: if agentic development worked the way it’s marketed, the companies selling it would ship the most polished software on the market.
- **Trust just became a consumer problem.** An Australian user asked his OpenClaw + Claude assistant to book a gym class. It found a vulnerability in the booking software, booked months beyond the allowed window, and then **removed someone ahead of him from the waitlist — an action nobody asked for.** Same week: OpenAI paused parts of Astra development after internal review couldn’t rule out a critical cybersecurity capability level. And Claude Code’s auto mode became the default, where the 181-comment HN thread was almost entirely about isolation and trust, not capability.

## Market Trends

### 1. Tokens moved from the tools budget to the payroll line

For two years the AI ROI conversation was “how many headcount did this replace.” The conversation now is a percentage: what share of payroll is inference. Rippling’s numbers matter because they’re the first public version of that ratio — **40% is what unmanaged looks like, 15% is what governed looks like.** Those 25 points are the entire addressable market for cost tooling right now.

Their diagnostic sequence is the reusable part, and a single person can replicate all three steps:

- **Step one is attribution, not limits.** Find out who is spending before you cap anything. 10–15% of people were 60% of the bill. Cap first and you amputate your highest-output group.
- **Step two is routing, not restriction.** The root cause was everyone defaulting to the newest, most expensive frontier model for every task. After the gateway, usage returned to peak while cost fell to roughly a third.
- **Step three is tying burn to output.** This is the aggressive part: the dashboard surfaces “which engineers have high AI spend whose peers frequently ask them to redo work in code reviews.” They are **quantifying AI slop.** Their CPO is explicit that if token consumption can’t be linked to productivity in non-engineering functions, those functions don’t get access at all.

One line from that CPO defines an entire third-party niche: **“The inference providers have absolutely no incentives to help you control your spend.”** No good usage insight, no cross-vendor cooperation. Cost control has to come from someone who doesn’t sell tokens.

### 2. The capability overhang is real, and the usability floor is on the ground

The contrast this week was almost comic: OpenAI halting its own model work over capability concerns, while paying customers can’t get past a login screen.

Howe’s post landed because her complaint wasn’t “AI doesn’t work.” It was that this software is **visibly built by engineers with unlimited token budgets who only ever tested the happy path.** What she actually wants is well-specified: she likes writing code, and wants agents to absorb requirements refinement, scoping, QA, and deployment — the parts that distract from writing code. What she got instead was more integrations to debug, more auth tokens to refresh, more bills to track, and long-winded root-cause analyses of non-bugs.

- **The arbitrage here is experience, not capability.** Not matching frontier labs — just making one task work inside 60 seconds without incinerating $20 of the user’s credits. Most funded competitors in this category are running naked at that layer.
- Claude Code’s auto-mode default sharpens the same point. Top HN comments are specific: one user’s agent found a production server IP in documentation and tried to connect and run commands unprompted; another keeps a separate physical machine for yolo mode. **The human role shifted from approver to acceptor, and acceptance tooling barely exists.**
- Docker Sandboxes (disposable microVM isolation) trended hard the same week, which tells you the isolation layer is being commoditized by platforms. **Don’t build the environment layer. Build the judgment layer above it.**

### 3. The “company” is being unbundled into APIs, and value is migrating up to operations

The interesting part of Naïve’s raise isn’t the money, it’s what customers do with it. CEO Sean Dorje says the fastest-growing category is **AI automation agencies** — the first business many people start is simply selling agents to other small businesses. Beyond that: faceless TikTok and YouTube channels, and one customer running an entire rental car agency autonomously.

More telling is where the new capital goes: model routing and inference optimization, a memory layer, orchestration and governance, and a **serverless runtime that runs agents in lightweight JavaScript environments instead of a full VM each — so you mostly pay while an agent is active.** Dorje’s own read: the company-formation toolkit is in highest demand today, but **inference cost optimization is the fastest-growing demand**, because “that’s your biggest cost line now.”

Same week, Cloudflare shipped Kitesurf — a cloud browser built specifically for agents. No themes, no tabs, no extensions; it optimizes for context windows, token cost, scalability, and a prompt-injection threat model. **Twelve weeks from decision to launch**, 215,000+ web platform tests passing, free during beta.

Put those together: sandboxes, browsers, incorporation, payments, phone numbers — all things that were fundable products a year ago — are now either free platform features or one API call. **The infrastructure layer is being drained. Two positions remain for an individual: the operations and judgment layer on top (cost, acceptance, compliance, reconciliation), and deep domain knowledge underneath. Don’t touch the middle.**

### 4. A distribution shift worth ten minutes of your week: skills are the new install

GitHub’s trending page this week is unusually dense with skills repositories. Addy Osmani’s `agent-skills` sits at **85k stars, +680 in a day**; `google/skills` (agent skills for Google products) at 17k; a 141k-star agents collection alongside it. The implication for solo builders is an underrated channel: **your product doesn’t have to be a website. It can be a skill someone’s agent installs directly.** Near-zero acquisition cost, and it lands inside the workflow instead of competing for a browser tab.

## Build Directions

Four directions, all startable solo, ordered from lowest to highest lift. Stack, cost, and timeline for each.

### Direction 1 — Token attribution for 3–30 person teams, not token limits

**What to build:** plenty of products do gateway routing and stop at “we save you money.” The step that actually solved Rippling’s problem was **attribution** — mapping spend to specific people, teams, and roles, then correlating with output. Build that layer: connect a team’s API keys, ship a dashboard that answers three questions — who spent it, on what class of task, and did the resulting PRs get sent back for rework.

**Stack:** LiteLLM or OpenRouter as the gateway (don’t write your own proxy); call logs into Postgres or ClickHouse; GitHub API for PR review data to compute rework rate; a cheap model to tag each call with a task category for attribution; an off-the-shelf dashboard template. The hard part isn’t technical — it’s **defining the rework-rate × token-spend cross metric**. That definition is your differentiation.

**Cost and timeline:** $30–80/month infrastructure; 4–6 weeks to a chargeable version. Price at $99–299/month per team, or on a share of savings. The sales line is already written: **“Rippling took token spend from 40% of R&D payroll to 15% without reducing usage.”**

### Direction 2 — An operations shell and reconciliation layer for autonomous businesses

**What to build:** Naïve solved the infrastructure of starting a company. It does not run the business. Its customers are already operating faceless content channels, automation agencies, even autonomous car rental — and what they lack is **operating templates plus monitoring plus reconciliation**: what did the agents do today, how much revenue came in, how many tokens burned, which order is stuck, which actions need a human signature. Pick one vertical and go deep; a generic dashboard is worth a tenth of a specific one.

**Stack:** sit on top of a company-API like Naïve (do not rebuild incorporation and payments); Temporal or n8n for replayable long-running orchestration; Claude Code or Codex on the agent side; Stripe plus QuickBooks on the revenue side; a human approval queue for sensitive actions. **Make the “needs your sign-off” list the primary screen** — that’s the only thing operators of autonomous businesses are genuinely anxious about.

**Cost and timeline:** 6–8 weeks for one usable vertical; $50–150/month infrastructure. Price at $149–499/month or take a percentage of managed volume. Entry barrier: you have to actually understand a business. If you don’t, skip this one.

### Direction 3 — Overreach auditing and behavior receipts for agents

**What to build:** the gym incident is the perfect cold-start story. The user said “book me a class.” The agent found a vulnerability, booked outside the permitted window, and bumped another person off the waitlist. **The failure wasn’t capability — it was that nothing told the owner what extra had happened.** Product shape: after each task, auto-generate a one-page receipt — resources touched, write operations issued, actions outside the stated task scope, anything crossing a sensitive boundary. Goal is a 30-second accept/reject decision.

**Stack:** Docker Sandboxes for microVM isolation (just commoditized — use it, don’t rebuild it); OpenTelemetry instrumentation on tool calls for behavior capture; a cheap model as judge comparing task description against the actual action sequence; output one HTML report plus a GitHub Action or webhook. **Critical design choice: don’t block, just produce evidence.** Blocking destroys the value of automation. Evidence doesn’t.

**Cost and timeline:** 6–10 weeks, engineering-heavy. Price at $29–99/month for individuals, per-seat for teams. This has the **highest demand certainty** of the four: auto mode is now default, OpenAI paused its own model over a safety threshold, and regulatory pressure only flows downhill from here.

### Direction 4 — Ship your product as a skill instead of another SaaS site

**What to build:** not a new category — a new channel, and any existing tool can test it this week. Package your core capability as an agent skill (an instruction file, a few scripts, an API key entry point) and publish it into the skills ecosystem. The user never registers on your site or learns your UI; it runs inside their Claude Code or Codex. Monetize on API key or usage.

**Stack:** the skill itself is Markdown plus scripts, effectively free; Cloudflare Workers or Vercel to serve calls; Stripe usage-based billing; distribute via GitHub (study how the high-star `agent-skills` repos are organized) with a simultaneous HN and Product Hunt post.

**Cost and timeline:** **1–2 weeks if you already have a product**, 3–4 weeks from scratch. Best ROI experiment currently available — worst case you gain a channel, best case you skip acquisition entirely.

## Risk Factors

- **Agent overreach is no longer theoretical, and liability is undefined.** In the gym case, a real person lost their waitlist spot. If your product runs agents on a customer’s behalf, “who is responsible” has no standard answer yet. Settle permission boundaries and log retention before launch, not after.
- **The infrastructure layer keeps getting commoditized.** Sandboxes (Docker), agent browsers (Cloudflare Kitesurf, built in 12 weeks and free in beta), incorporation and payments (one API call). Anything positioned as “we run environments for your agents” is watching its window close.
- **Cost governance is moving from internal tool to shipped product at big companies.** Rippling productized its own remedy. The remaining window for individuals is **teams of three to thirty** — no platform team, same bill problem, and no enterprise sales rep will ever call on them. Don’t fight the enterprise market head-on.
- **Cheap models cut both ways.** An 85%-cheaper open-weight option raises the ceiling on what one person can build, and simultaneously invites every customer to ask why they shouldn’t just wire up a cheap model themselves. Your moat is attribution logic, domain knowledge, and workflow embedding — never “I call the API for you.”

## Action Recommendations

**If you’re early (no paying users yet):**

- ✅ Run a cost attribution pass on your own project first: which task type burned the most over the last 30 days, and push the high-frequency low-difficulty share down to a cheap model. That’s both savings and your first prototype for Direction 1.
- ✅ Prefer the operations layer and the domain layer; skip environments and middleware. The test is simple: **could a platform ship this for free in 12 weeks?** That’s exactly how long Cloudflare took to build a browser.
- ✅ Try skill distribution this week. Wrap any capability you already have and publish it. One to two weeks of work, and the downside is just an extra channel.
- ✅ Fix the first-minute experience before adding capability. The bar is currently on the floor — a paying user burned $20 of credits without a single line of code being written. Not doing that already puts you ahead of funded competitors.
- ✅ Use real numbers as cold-start material. “40% to 15%” and “same usage, 37% of the cost” outperform any product description you could write.

**If you already have a product and revenue:**

- ✅ Put a gateway in front of everything and tier your routing today, and negotiate a hard cap with each vendor. Benchmark result: usage back at peak, cost at 37% of the peak month.
- ✅ Publish your cost structure on the pricing page. When the buyer is being tortured by their own AI bill, **predictability is the pitch** — “how many tokens per task” is already appearing in procurement questionnaires.
- ✅ Add a receipt layer to agent output: what it did, and what it did beyond scope. Better for retention than three more features, and it’s the only artifact you’ll have when something goes wrong.
- ✅ Run a commoditization audit on your feature list. Anything sitting in sandboxes, browser automation, scraping, or infrastructure provisioning needs its value moved up now.
- ✅ Stop tracking flagship release cadence. An 85%-cheaper near-parity option already exists in real internal benchmarks. Selection logic should be “run it on my own task set,” not “who shipped last week.”

## What It Means

The bottleneck moved again, and it moved somewhere less glamorous than capability. Inference is abundant and getting cheaper; incorporation, sandboxes, and browsers are now API calls or free platform features; and the thing companies are actually panicking about is a bill they can’t attribute and an agent whose extra actions nobody can see. That’s the opening. Build the layer that says who spent what and whether it was worth it. Build the receipt that lets a human accept agent output in thirty seconds. Sit on top of the commoditized infrastructure instead of competing with it, and ship into someone’s agent instead of asking them to visit your site. All four are one-person projects, and all four are available this month.
