## Market Trends

### 1. The review layer is the open category, and it’s opening all at once

When five unrelated teams ship the same missing piece in one week, that’s a category forming. Execlave gates what agents can touch in the real world. Phinq intercepts destructive actions pre-execution. Cadre.rocks turns agent runs into a kanban board with blockers, plan review, diff review and explicit approval before anything ships. Soloop brands itself an “approval-first Agent OS” aimed specifically at solo founders. MarkupBase’s whitepaper makes the structural argument: raw logs are built for forensics, not for timely review, and a generic “Allow agent to continue?” prompt gives the reviewer essentially zero control.

- **Why now:** agents crossed from answering to acting. Once an agent can move money, email a customer, or push to prod, “it worked” and “I’d have approved it” stop being the same statement.
- **The insight to steal:** the deliverable of an agent is no longer output — it’s a *reviewable* artifact. Versioned Markdown and static HTML beat log streams because a human can actually assess them in two minutes.

### 2. Litt’s point cuts against full autonomy — and it’s the commercially useful read

Litt’s argument is subtle and worth getting right, because the shallow version (“review your agent’s code”) is already priced in. His claim: agents are getting genuinely good at *verifying* their own work, so verification is not why you should understand the code. You understand it **to participate** — a project is many loops, and your fluency with the system is what generates the next idea. Lose that and you can still ship, but you stop steering.

- **For a one-person company this is existential**, not philosophical. You are the only source of direction. The moment you can’t hold the system in your head, your roadmap degrades to whatever the agent suggests next.
- **Practical shape:** his techniques — agent-generated explainer docs, self-quizzes to test comprehension, small interactive “micro-worlds” to poke at the system — are all cheap to automate now. That is a product, not just a habit.

### 3. Tune the harness, not the model — the cheapest capability gain left

A Show HN this cycle, `harness-training`, formalizes something most solo builders do by accident. It freezes the model and trains the *harness* around it — prompts, context management, tool definitions, repair loop — in a PyTorch-shaped loop where each epoch proposes one diff to a single policy file, measures it against a task panel, and promotes or rejects via git. Candidates live under `refs/candidates/`, so the promotion history *is* `git log`. Their result: train the harness once, freeze it, and general capability lifts across different underlying models.

Same theme elsewhere: Airbnb engineering published its **eval-driven development** practice for GenAI at scale, and Rails shipped a “Rails is built for AI” positioning. The pattern is consistent — teams stopped chasing model swaps and started engineering the scaffold, because scaffolding transfers when the model changes and model choice doesn’t transfer at all.

- **For solopreneurs:** you get frontier-model behavior out of cheap models by investing in the harness. Given Gemini 3.7 Flash at $0.75/M, harness quality is now worth more than model tier for most workloads.

### 4. The abundance has a shadow side: injection, promo expiry, vendor evaporation

Three unrelated stories this cycle are really one story about fragility. Meanwhile CME Group is listing **compute futures from October 5**, which tells you the market itself expects GPU pricing to stay volatile enough to hedge.

- **Read for builders:** every one of those is a single-point-of-failure story. Cheap generation tempts you to build thin layers on top of other people’s continuity. Own your artifacts locally, assume any input can be adversarial, and don’t underwrite your margin with someone else’s promotional price.

## Build Directions (what to build + the exact stack)

**① The review surface for agent output**  
Not another dashboard. Take the raw output of an agent run — diffs, actions taken, records changed, evidence gathered — and render it as a versioned, human-readable artifact a reviewer can clear in under two minutes: what changed, what scope it touched, what’s unresolved, what breaks if it’s wrong. MarkupBase argues the thesis; nobody owns the tool yet.

- **Stack:** GitHub App + webhooks (capture agent runs) → generate versioned Markdown/static HTML artifacts → Postgres for version history → comment threads anchored to passages. Deploy on Vercel; Resend for review notifications.
- **Cost / cycle:** 2–3 weeks to a usable MVP, hosting under $30/mo. Price $19–49/seat/mo. Wedge in on Claude Code / Codex users who already feel the diff-review pain.

**② Guardrail gateway between agents and the real world**  
The Execlave/Phinq shape, but built vertically instead of generically: one gateway that mediates a specific dangerous surface — payments, email-to-customer, production database writes. Scoped credentials, allow-lists, dry-run preview, hard stop on destructive verbs, full audit trail. Sell the audit trail as much as the block.

- **Stack:** MCP proxy server in front of your tool calls + Open Policy Agent (or plain declarative rules) for policy + Cloudflare Workers at the edge + append-only audit log in Postgres/S3. Short-lived scoped tokens, never long-lived keys.
- **Cost / cycle:** 3–4 weeks. Prices well because it’s a compliance line item, not a productivity tool: $99–499/mo. Pick one surface and be the best at it.

**③ Harness tuning / eval-as-a-service for small teams**  
`harness-training` proved the loop; Airbnb proved the demand at the other end of the market. Package it: point it at someone’s agent setup, build a task panel from their real workloads, and iterate their prompts/tools/context policy against measured pass rates — then hand back a frozen harness that also works on cheaper models.

- **Stack:** promptfoo or Braintrust for eval scaffolding (or roll your own on a Terminal-Bench-style panel) + git-based candidate promotion + Docker task sandboxes + Claude Code or Codex as the diff proposer.
- **Cost / cycle:** productized service, 1–2 weeks per engagement, $2k–8k per harness plus retainer. Highest-margin direction on this list because the output is measurable (“pass rate 61% → 84%, token cost −40%”).

**④ Capacity arbitrage tooling and advisory**  
`cookbook-meter` measured $7,377 of API-equivalent work on a $200 plan. Almost nobody knows their own number. Build the team version: aggregate subscription-vs-API-equivalent across Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, per project and per person, surface where you’re over-buying API and under-using subscription capacity — or the reverse.

- **Stack:** local transcript parsers (Claude Code writes JSONL to disk; keep it local-first, it’s a trust product) + per-model pricing table + Stripe + Vercel. Ship the CLI free and open-source, charge for the team rollup.

Three simultaneous price/latency events (Gemini 3.7 Flash at half price, Ultrafast at 750 tok/s, BDH-CQ at $0.007/task) mean the optimal route changes monthly. ## Risk Factors

> The window is genuinely open. These are the four ways builders will lose money in it.

- **Promo pricing is not a business model.** Gemini 3.7 Flash’s half-price is explicitly promotional through end of 2026. If your unit economics only work at $0.75/M, you have a repricing event on your calendar whether you planned one or not.
- **Injection now arrives inside legitimate documents.** A hidden instruction in a legal filing told the AI to side with the filer. Any agent that ingests untrusted PDFs, emails or tickets is an attack surface — and “the model was careful” is not a control.
- **Review becomes the human bottleneck.** Generation went 37× cheaper; your attention did not. Ship agents faster than you can review them and you accumulate cognitive debt: a system you own, ship on, and no longer understand well enough to steer.
- **Vendor evaporation is real, not theoretical.** PBS lost 70 years of archives when a storage vendor went defunct; Namecheap dropped for six hours on a cooling failure. Keep local copies of anything that is your actual asset, and don’t build the whole company inside one other company’s uptime.

## What it means

The economics inverted this week. For two years the scarce thing was generation — tokens, context, throughput — and the winning move was getting more of it, cheaper. That fight is effectively over: one founder now pulls $7,377 of work out of a $200 subscription, and the price floor keeps falling. What’s scarce now is *judgment applied at speed* — the ability to look at what an agent did, understand it well enough to steer the next loop, and clear it or stop it before it touches the real world.

That’s why the review layer, the guardrail gateway, and the harness are the three things worth building right now. They all sell the same underlying good: confidence that autonomous work is correct and contained. The founders who spend this quarter building yet another wrapper on cheap inference will be competing with a commodity that gets cheaper every three weeks. The ones who build the approval layer are selling the thing that just became expensive.
