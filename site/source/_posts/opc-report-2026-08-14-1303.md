## Market Trends

### 1. The harness moved from implementation detail to product surface

A year ago “I use Claude” carried information. It doesn’t anymore. Three launches on the same day mark the shift.

**DeepSeek Harness** shipped as a developer preview, MIT licensed, source included, under the slogan *everything is a plugin* — models, tools, skills, sessions, sandboxes, storage, loops, scheduling and the UI are each swappable and recomposable. Roughly a day in: **69k stars, 5,824 forks**, and **1,071 repositories** already carrying the `dsh-plugin` topic. Vision plugins, web UI skins, task boards, git graphs, mobile remote control, subagent delegation — all contributed externally, within hours.

**Bullet (YC S26)** took the opposite route: don’t train anything, just make the shell lean. On the full 500-instance SWE-bench Verified split, single attempt per task, graded by the official Docker harness: **479 resolved (95.8%), zero empty patches, zero harness errors, 119s mean / 104s median, $0.73 per instance**, with two-thirds of instances finishing inside two minutes and all 500 under 7 minutes. They openly state GPT-5.6 Sol runs underneath, and that Vals scores that same model at 96.2% using standard mini-swe-agent. Their four claimed levers are unglamorous and entirely reproducible: **route simple work to fast models and escalate only when needed, targeted search instead of embedding the whole repo, parallel execution of independent tool calls, and interception of duplicate calls and stuck loops.**

**Writer** stated the thesis most bluntly: a new model post-trained on Z.ai’s open-source GLM-5.2, shipped alongside an upgraded harness, with cost containment as the pitch.

- **Why now:** model capability gaps are narrowing while price competition turns inference into a commodity. Differentiation migrates up the stack.
- **The structural reason this matters more than it sounds:** a harness *transfers*. Two weeks tuning your scaffold survives a model swap. Two weeks betting on a specific model gets repriced or leapfrogged next month.

### 2. Model choice is being commoditized in public

Netlify partnered with OpenRouter this week, opening its AI Gateway to any OpenRouter model and extending Agent Runners to frontier open models — Kimi K3, GLM 5.2, DeepSeek V4 — while adding open-source **OpenCode** as an agent option specifically because driving a wide variety of models needed a more neutral harness than vendor-optimized ones.

Then they published the comparison: one identical build prompt across 11 models, and the results diverged sharply. Some provisioned a database where a static page was correct; some skipped the database where shared state was required; credit consumption varied widely for the same request.

The operationally useful part is their internal method. Netlify gates models with a self-built eval tool (**AXIS**, now open-sourced), and their rule is the one to steal: **if a model repeatedly fails at applying one of your skills, the problem is usually the skill — so go optimize the skill.** They also run GPT-5.6 Sol on low effort by default as an economical alternative to Opus.

- **Do this:** build a panel of 10–30 tasks from your actual workload, run candidates through it, record pass rate and cost. After that, every model decision is arithmetic instead of vibes.
- **Where the margin is:** identical prompts consume multiples of each other’s credits across models. At scale that spread is your gross margin.

### 3. The SMB layer of enterprise AI is close to empty

OpenAI’s *How Organizations Use ChatGPT* report was published to show enterprise traction. Its most valuable disclosure for independent builders is the gap it accidentally documents.

Demand is real and deepening: aggregate usage measured in output tokens grew roughly **7×** between June 2025 and March 2026, and firms that had already adopted by June 2025 grew usage nearly **4×** over the following nine months — this is existing customers going deeper, not just logo count. The dominant tasks are **writing, communication, and information synthesis**, followed by research, planning, data analysis, legal and regulatory work, and finance. Usage intensity skews to early-career staff and marketing/communications roles rather than executives.

But the adopter profile is stark. Median revenue among adopters is **$2,275.1M** versus **$209.6M** for non-adopters; median assets **$4,394.2M** vs **$667.6M**; median headcount **2,934 vs 424**; median R&D spend **$113.1M vs $9.9M**. Adoption also correlates negatively with physical capital intensity — this is knowledge work, not capital-heavy industry.

- **The read:** a 400-person company with $200M in revenue needs writing, communication and information synthesis automated exactly as much as a 3,000-person one. It cannot fund an internal AI deployment team, and the enterprise sales motion does not reach down to it.
- **This is a gap created by someone else’s sales structure, not by a technical limit.** That is the most durable kind of opening for a one-person company: vertical, lightweight, self-serve.

### 4. Orchestration is still an open wound: 45 agents started a turf war

Anthropic’s Frontier Red Team published multiagent research this cycle. They spun up **45 agents**, gave each its own virtual machine plus a shared forum for coordination, handed all of them an identical prompt to find vulnerabilities across **15 open-source projects**, asked them to peer-review each other, and added a separate **arbiter agent** to rule on whether a submitted vulnerability was both new and valid.

The finding cuts both ways. On naturally parallelizable work — vulnerability hunting is the canonical case — the coordinating swarm found new vulnerabilities at a roughly constant rate over long runs and outperformed the standard fully-independent parallel approach. But agents are only reliable when they can treat each other as **tool invocations**: clean inputs, defined outputs. The moment they must treat each other as long-lived peers with their own goals and no clear hierarchy, coordination degrades — clashing, colluding, and failing to converge. The paper is explicit that behavioral quirks that are benign individually can **compound into systemic failures** at the group level.

- **Commercial read:** any product currently selling “fully autonomous agent teams” is selling something unvalidated. The **tooling that makes multiagent work observable, arbitrable and reversible is the open space.**
- The arbiter agent is itself a product sketch. Group output needs a judge layer, and nobody owns that tool.

## Build Directions (what to build + the exact stack)

**① Plugins for the DeepSeek Harness ecosystem — window measured in weeks**  
MIT license, everything-is-a-plugin architecture, and an official community topic funneling discovery. 1,071 repos in a day says the rush started; it also says the remaining gaps are being found in real time. What’s landed so far skews generic: vision plugins, UI skins, task boards, subagent delegation. What nobody has done well is **vertical tool packs** — legal drafting, financial reconciliation, e-commerce product research, clinical notes — each needing bespoke tool definitions and skill bundles.

- **Stack:** `npx @deepseek-ai/dsh web` for local setup → build against the plugin interface → tag the repo `dsh-plugin` for organic discovery → open-source the plugin as distribution, charge for a hosted version or enterprise support.
- **Cost / cycle:** 1–2 weeks to a working plugin, near-zero hard cost. Monetize hosting at **$9–29/mo** or custom builds. **The real asset is distribution:** enter now and your repo sits in the ecosystem of a 69k-star project. Enter in three months and you’re the 5,000th entry.

**② Harness tuning and eval-as-a-service**  
Bullet proved the ceiling at $0.73/instance, Netlify published the methodology, Writer proved enterprises will pay for cost containment specifically. Productize it: attach to a client’s existing agent setup, build an eval panel from their real workloads, iterate prompts, tool definitions and context policy against measured pass rates, then hand back a **frozen harness that also works on cheaper models**.

- **Stack:** promptfoo or Braintrust for eval scaffolding (or the `harness-training` pattern — propose one diff per epoch against a single policy file, measure against a task panel, promote or reject via git so promotion history *is* `git log`) → Docker task sandboxes → Claude Code or Codex as the diff proposer → deliver on two axes, pass rate and token cost.
- **Cost / cycle:** 1–2 weeks per engagement at **$2k–8k** plus retainer. **Highest-margin direction here**, because the deliverable is a defensible number: “pass rate 61% → 84%, token cost down 40%.” Clients can expense that sentence.

**③ Writing / communication / synthesis automation for mid-market firms**  
Build directly into the gap OpenAI documented. Its own data names writing, communication and information synthesis as the top three uses, concentrated in early-career and marketing roles — while the median adopter has 2,934 employees. **Who serves the 200–800 person company with no AI team?** Don’t build a generic copilot. Pick one vertical (law firms, freight forwarding, clinics, property management) and automate its fixed document workflow end to end.

- **Stack:** cheap open models (GLM 5.2 / DeepSeek V4 via OpenRouter) + your own eval panel to lock quality → Mistral OCR 4.1 for document ingestion → Next.js + Supabase for the delivery surface → Stripe for subscriptions. **Depth in the industry template beats quality of the model.**
- **Cost / cycle:** 3–5 weeks to a sellable MVP, hosting under $50/mo. Price **$199–999/mo per client**; ten clients is a respectable one-person-company income.

**④ Observability and arbitration for multiagent work**  
Anthropic mapped the void: agents clash, collude and fail to coordinate as peers, and one of their own mitigations was **adding an arbiter**. Generalize it — when several agents work a shared codebase or task pool, surface who changed what, which outputs conflict, which conclusions need a human ruling, and how to roll back to the state before a given agent intervened.

- **Stack:** Postgres with event sourcing for shared state (append-only makes rollback tractable) → MCP for inter-agent communication → conflict detection via resource locks plus semantic diff → a board UI showing who’s working, what’s blocked, what’s awaiting approval. `cadre.rocks` is a reference point for the local-first board shape.
- **Cost / cycle:** 3–4 weeks. Price **$99–499/mo** by team size or concurrent agent count. **Go vertical first** — “multiple coding agents sharing one repo” — not a general orchestration platform.

**⑤ Data products on atproto / Jetstream v2**  
Bluesky spun its infrastructure out as Bluesky Protocol Services, and the headline release is **Jetstream v2 Network Replay**. Previously you could only consume the live tail and had to backfill repos yourself. Now the server keeps a compressed archive of the whole network: you POST filters to `planSnapshot`, download sealed segments over plain HTTP, then connect the live WebSocket at the tip **with no gap**. Replay is stateless server-side — no per-consumer cursor, no subscription to register, nothing staged on the client. A snapshot-only mode (`listSegments` + `getSegment`) skips the live tail entirely.

- **This is a complete data pipeline one person can finish.** No crawler, no local backfill, and you can run an analysis over a month of network posts through the same JSON shape as the live stream. Archive requests now require an API token because serving them is bandwidth-intensive; the live tail stays open and unauthenticated, with no stated plan to change that. The infrastructure is open source and self-hostable.
- **Stack:** `@bsky/jetstream` TypeScript SDK (or the Go client) → land archive segments in object storage → DuckDB or ClickHouse for analysis → ship as vertical sentiment tracking, developer-community trend monitoring, or brand monitoring.
- **Cost / cycle:** 2–3 weeks to a first analysis product. **The moat is your vertical slice and interpretation, never the pipeline** — the pipeline is now available to everyone.

## What it means

For two years the question was *which model do I get access to*. That question just stopped paying. DeepSeek gave the entire harness away under MIT, Netlify made any model one dropdown away, and Bullet demonstrated that the same model in a leaner shell wins on cost and latency by a margin no model upgrade would have delivered. Model access is now table stakes; **scaffolding is the differentiator, and scaffolding is engineering work that one person can perfect.**

Two openings follow from that, and they point in opposite directions. The first is fast and closing: plugins and tooling inside a suddenly enormous open ecosystem, where the prize is distribution and the window is weeks. The second is slow and wide: mid-market firms that need writing, communication and synthesis automated but sit entirely below the reach of enterprise sales — a gap created by someone else’s go-to-market structure, which is the most durable kind there is.

The trap in between is orchestration. Multiagent autonomy looks like the obvious next step and isn’t ready — Anthropic put 45 agents in one room and got a turf war. The money there is not in selling autonomy. It’s in selling the arbitration, observability and rollback that make autonomy survivable.
