# Solo Founder Revenue Splits in Two: Median Down 23%, Top Decile Up 19%, Gap Now 61x

**September 2, 2026**

AI raised the ceiling on what one person can build. It did not raise the floor. Those two facts, held together, explain almost everything happening in solo business right now — and they point at a very different set of build decisions than the ones that worked eighteen months ago.

---

## Key Insights

The headline number is uncomfortable. Among solo-founded companies incorporated through Stripe Atlas in 2025, **median first-six-month revenue fell 23% year over year, while top-decile revenue rose 19%**. The gap between the median and the top ten percent widened from 34x four years ago to **61x**. Same market, same tools, same six-month window, opposite directions.

This is not “AI is killing indie hackers.” It is a relocation of where value sits. Shipping software stopped being scarce. What became scarce is everything shipping used to be bundled with: proprietary data, entry into workflows that have no API, distribution that cannot be cloned, and the operational discipline to keep an inference bill from eating a margin.

Four numbers that should change what you do this month:

- **2.3x** — revenue multiple for AI-native solo companies at month 24 versus other solo companies. The gap compounds: 1.2x at six months, 1.5x at twelve, 2.3x at twenty-four. “AI-native” means the product does not exist without the model — not that you bolted on a chat box.
- **51% vs 2%** — share of revenue from international customers, top decile versus median. Top performers reach 10 countries in month one; the median reaches 3. This is the widest gap in the dataset and also the cheapest one to close, because it is a decision, not a capability.
- **30% vs 8%** — month-two customer return rate, top decile versus median. Customers acquired in month one spend 47% more by year two. Retention is the most under-priced compounding asset in solo business.
- **$60–200 per seat per month** — the real cost of running coding agents daily, against a $10–20 headline price. Per-token prices are falling and bills are still rising, because agents burn tokens faster than prices drop.

## Market Trends

### Value moved from the model to the harness

The most expensive lesson of 2026 was learned in enterprise procurement, but it applies directly to a one-person operation: the model is not the expensive part anymore. The wrapper around it is.

One IT consulting firm paid roughly $200,000 for 800 Cursor licences over a year. The renewal ask came in at approximately $1.5 million. They pushed back and settled near $250,000. Sanofi and Druva both faced renewals around five times the prior year, and Sanofi’s chief digital officer said publicly: “I may not renew with Cursor. I get a better deal with Claude Code.”

The counter-moves are just as instructive. Sapiom — a startup Anthropic itself backed — swapped in the open-source OpenCode harness and expects to cut AI spend 25%. Codestrap, a coding consultancy, built its own routing layer sending most tasks to small Google and OpenAI models and reports customer costs fell 97%. Two quotes from that reporting frame the thesis: “It’s not about the model anymore. It’s about the harness around the model,” and “All the value is accruing to the harness.”

Here is what the four major coding agents actually cost as of September 2026:

| Tool | Entry | Heavy daily use | Billing mechanic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GitHub Copilot | Pro $10/mo (incl. $15 credits) | Pro+ $39, Max $100 | Usage-based AI credits since June 1, 2026 (1 credit = $0.01); completions stay free |
| Claude Code | Claude Pro $20/mo | Max $100 (5x) or $200 (20x) | Session capacity on a rolling 5-hour window; hard ceiling, no overage surprise |
| Cursor | Pro $20/mo | Pro+ $60, Ultra $200 | Dollar credit pool; cheap light months, surprising heavy ones |
| Codex | Bundled with ChatGPT plans | OpenAI estimates ~$100–200/dev/mo | Token-based credits |

The pattern: entry pricing has converged around $10–20 and become close to meaningless. The decision is made at the “I use this all day” tier, and it hinges on whether you want a predictable ceiling (Claude Code) or a cheap floor with variance (Cursor, Copilot).

**What to do with this:** if your product is a thin layer on someone else’s model, you are standing exactly where the repricing is happening. The harness layer — routing, evals, observability, context management — is where value is currently pooling. But read the next section before you build there.

### The general-purpose agent OS lane is already full

August’s Product Hunt leaderboard reads like a pile-up in a single lane. Soloop (approval-first agent OS for solo founders, ~475 votes). Coldtea.ai (self-driving software delivery, 513). Cloudflare OS (open-source enterprise AI operating system, 460). AgentSky (any harness, any model, cloud-hosted agents, 430). Construct Computer took #1 Product of the Day on August 23. Also Omniwork, HAR (open-source multi-agent coding harness), and Maritime — dedicated persistent VMs for agents at **$1 per agent per month**. On the open-source side, Munder Difflin wrapped 12 command-line agents into an “office of your clones” and passed 10,000 users and 30,000 spawned agents within days.

Two conclusions, one bad and one good.

The bad one: if your plan is a general-purpose “operating system for the one-person company,” you are late, and one of your competitors is Cloudflare.

The good one: the substrate got cheap enough to treat as a utility. A dollar a month buys an isolated VM with a browser, a filesystem, and persistent state. That means “give every customer their own dedicated agent” now has a near-zero marginal cost. Infrastructure is not the thing to build. It is the thing to build *on*.

### Workflows without APIs just opened up

On August 5, Hark previewed Handoff, scoring **97.7 on the Online-Mind2Web benchmark** against ChatGPT 5.4 at 92.8 and Claude Opus 4.8 at 84.1, at claimed token costs under one-tenth of comparable frontier products. The mechanism matters more than the score: Handoff spins up a dedicated virtual computer per task — browser, filesystem, terminal — and interacts with sites the way a person does, reading the interface and clicking. So it works on systems with **no public API at all**: government portals, vendor back-offices, ancient industry software.

It is not alone. Cloudflare shipped Kitesurf, an agent-first browser on Workers. BrowserOS neo and Rindler both landed in the same window, the latter specifically framed around automating “the web work your team does by hand,” including signing in and repairing workflows when pages change.

**Why this matters more for a one-person business than for anyone else:** “this industry has no API” used to be a wall that killed automation projects. It is now an entry point. The dirtier the process and the more absent the API, the less likely a big vendor’s integration team will go there — and the less likely a competitor with a weekend and Cursor will bother either.

### Enterprises are paying for data and workflow, not for wrappers

Agentic AI M&A went from 9 deals to 35 in twelve months. What buyers bought tells you what the market prices:

- Asana paid **$75M** for StackAI (~55 people) — for cross-system execution, explicitly not a chatbot.
- Salesforce agreed to **$3.6B** for Fin (formerly Intercom), whose agent resolves an average of 76% of support volume end to end.
- Zendesk acquired Forethought, valuing among other things its ability to extend into enterprise systems **where APIs do not exist**.
- Coupa bought document understanding. Vertice bought a procurement pricing dataset. Salesforce also bought m3ter, a usage-metering company — because outcome-based billing needs a meter.

a16z estimates 30–40% of the roughly $450B vertical SaaS market gets reshaped by agents between 2026 and 2028. Gartner projects 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific agents by end of 2026, up from under 5% in 2025.

The inverse is the more actionable half. Acquirers have stopped buying API wrappers — a thin UI over someone else’s foundation model, with no proprietary data and no workflow integration, has become close to worthless as an acquisition target because anyone can rebuild it in weeks. What commands premiums: specialized data, models tuned to a specific workflow, and distribution that would take years to recreate.

## Build Directions

### 1. Agent-native vertical services — sell the outcome, not the software

**What:** pick a narrow industry with messy process, real compliance weight, and an obvious budget owner. Let agents run delivery; a human signs off. The template already exists — Last Accounting Company (YC S26) is an agent-native accounting service for Finnish startups and SMBs where agents keep books current and human accountants validate filings. Transferable entry points: licence and permit filings, first-pass insurance claims, cross-border VAT compliance, vendor onboarding review, clinical documentation.

**Stack:** Claude Code or Codex to build the internal system ($20–100/mo) + a computer-use agent for portals with no API (Handoff, Browserbase, Kitesurf) + persistent agent VMs in the Maritime mould ($1/agent/mo) + self-hosted n8n for orchestration + Stripe for billing.

**Cost and timeline:** $150–400/mo, 6–10 weeks to first paying customer. Price per outcome — per filing, per case — not per seat. That pricing choice is where most of the margin lives.

### 2. A harness for exactly one workflow

**What:** do not build a general platform; that lane is jammed. Build the harness for **one** workflow: routing, an eval set, retry logic, context trimming, a cost dashboard. “Contract review harness.” “Ecommerce listing harness.” The value proof is already public and quantified — 25% saved at Sapiom, 97% at Codestrap — and enterprise buyers are actively shopping alternatives after 5x renewal quotes.

**Stack:** OpenCode or HAR as the harness base (open source) + OpenRouter or ngrok AI Gateway for model routing + your own eval set, which is the actual asset + tracing for observability.

**Cost and timeline:** $50–150/mo, 4–8 weeks to a demoable version. Price against the percentage of the bill you eliminate. That is the easiest sentence to sell in this market.

### 3. B2B, global from day one, recurring — the boring one that works

**What:** not a category, a set of constraints, taken straight from the measured difference between top decile and median. B2B beats B2C even when bootstrapped. Sell globally from week one — 51% international versus 2% is the widest gap in the data. Bill recurring. Win retention in month one. Apply these four to *any* direction and the expected value changes.

**Stack:** Lovable or Cursor to build ($20–60/mo) + Supabase for backend + Stripe with **Stripe Tax enabled** (automatic VAT and sales tax by customer location — the single most common thing that stops “global from day one” from actually happening) + Make or n8n for automation.

**Cost and timeline:** $75–150/mo. Tooling spend is a rounding error here. The real constraint is whether you are willing to charge in week one.

### 4. Build a dataset nobody else can get

**What:** acquirers pay premiums for proprietary data, workflow-tuned models, and distribution that takes years to rebuild. So systematically record one thing in your industry that nobody has structured: actual approval-time distributions for a permit class, real transaction price bands in a vertical, denial-reason distributions for a claim type, platform policy change history. Ship a thin tool whose job is to accumulate the data. **The dataset is the deliverable.**

**Stack:** browser agents for continuous collection (API where one exists, computer-use where it does not) + scheduled jobs + a light query interface. Vertice buying Vendr and Coupa buying Rossum are the exit logic made explicit.

**Cost and timeline:** $100–250/mo. Value increases monotonically with time, and it is the one asset the next model generation will not flatten.

### 5. Retention-first narrow wedge

**What:** the 30% versus 8% month-two return rate is the most changeable number in the dataset. The method is counterintuitive: **deliver manually until retention exists, then automate**. Charge in week one even if it is small. Onboard the first ten customers yourself. Turn what a customer says into this week’s product change with zero latency — that direct line from pain to shipped change is the only structural advantage one person has over a team.

**Stack:** embarrassingly little. A payment link, a shared doc, a calendar. Add tooling after retention holds.

**Cost and timeline:** $20–50/mo, validated in four weeks. The only direction on this list that requires discipline rather than technical judgment.

## Risk Factors

- **The builder-to-builder market is saturated.** $9–49/month utilities sold to other builders are the first layer to break, because the buyer can rebuild your product in a weekend and the pool is small and self-referential. Walk toward real industries, not toward your peers.
- **Thin SEO is closed.** Keyword pages waiting on free Google signups no longer work; answers now happen inside the chat window. First-hand, decision-shaped content that models cite still performs — but it is one pipe, not the whole plumbing.
- **Token bills move opposite to token prices.** Unit prices fall while autonomous agents consume faster, so the bill climbs. Without routing and context discipline, a $20 plan becomes a $200 reality. Every publicly documented overrun traces back to context hygiene or quota monitoring.
- **Browser agent security is unanswered.** A VM with full browser access — live sessions, saved passwords, connected accounts — has no published answer for how an enterprise security team should review it. Wire one into a client’s accounts and the liability is yours.
- **Approval fatigue turns human-in-the-loop into theatre.** The sharpest question asked of Soloop on Product Hunt: “How do you stop approval from becoming a habit? A founder who approves everything is reading nothing.” Design by stakes — a $50 test and a pivot must not share the same interface.

## Action Recommendations

**If you are still choosing a direction (0 to 1)**

- Run your candidate directions through one filter this week: *does this industry have an API?* Prefer the one that does not. That is the last unfilled space.
- Charge in week one. The amount is irrelevant; willingness to pay is the only signal that does not lie.
- Copy, pricing, and checkout in English and global by default, with Stripe Tax on. That is the concrete version of closing the 51%-versus-2% gap.
- Start on Copilot Pro at $10 or Claude Pro at $20. Do not buy Ultra or Max before your actual usage demands it.
- Onboard your first ten customers by hand. You are buying the 30% month-two retention.

**If you already have something running (1 to N)**

- Compute your per-customer token unit economics this week — monthly burn and gross margin per account. Then add routing; simple tasks to small models. Documented savings run from 25% to 97%.
- Answer honestly: could a customer rebuild your core value with Claude Code over a weekend? If yes, your moat has to become data, distribution, or privileged access — not features.
- Put the hours AI saved you **entirely into distribution**. Spending the surplus polishing the same features is accelerating into a wall.
- Migrate pricing from per-seat toward per-outcome. Enterprises are already buying this way — Salesforce bought a metering company to support it. Moving early reads as understanding your customer’s economics.
- Start accumulating a proprietary dataset. It is the only asset on this list that appreciates and survives the next model release.

## What It Means

The tooling advantage is spent. When everyone can build, building stops being the differentiator — and the evidence is right there in a median that fell 23% while the top decile rose 19% using the same tools in the same year.

What replaced it is unglamorous and harder to copy: a vertical nobody wants to touch, a workflow with no API, a dataset that takes eighteen months to accumulate, a customer relationship where the distance between complaint and shipped fix is measured in hours. None of it is exciting. All of it is durable.

The direction to walk is away from the crowd building tools for the crowd, and toward the parts of the economy that will never write their own software. The tool to walk with is whatever costs least while your unit economics are still unproven — and a routing layer the day they are.
